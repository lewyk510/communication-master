LANE — authored natively, NOT a translation

This lane collects what English speakers actually say to themselves: the critic's favorite lines, the pre-meeting jitters, the 3 a.m. replay, and the wording swaps that have evidence behind them. The "responses" here are inward-facing: you are both speaker and listener. Mechanisms and evidence live in [[concepts/c14-self-talk/core]].

### "I always mess this up"

**Context** — Right after a visible failure: a rejected proposal, a fumbled answer, an email sent with a typo. Loud when you're alone or scrolling other people's wins.

**Reading** — (1) Overgeneralization: one event is being promoted into a verdict about you; (2) A borrowed voice, usually a parent's or an old boss's phrasing; (3) Preemptive self-punishment to beat others to the punch.

**Response options**
1. Name + scope: "Maya, you messed up this one, not everything."
2. Behavior-only rewrite: "This draft missed the point" instead of "I'm hopeless."
3. Friend test: would you say this sentence to a friend? If not, rewrite it the way you'd say it to them.

**Pitfalls** — Self-criticism triggers avoidance, avoidance creates fresh evidence, the loop deepens. Beating yourself up is not a discipline strategy.

**Examples** — constructed example: after the proposal came back, two hours of "useless" and zero pages written; switching to "the logic didn't land, add the data" produced a second draft in forty minutes.

### "Alex, you've done harder things."

**Context** — The final minute before a talk, an interview, a hard conversation. Saying your own name feels odd, which is exactly the point: it flags that you're doing this deliberately.

**Reading** — (1) Ordinary pep talk; (2) Deliberate distanced self-talk, recruiting the observer view you'd use on anyone else; (3) Fragile bravado. Check the second half: a concrete fact marks (2), a bare slogan marks (3).

**Response options**
1. Name + evidence: "Alex, you rehearsed three times and the numbers check out."
2. Name + worst case: "Alex, worst case they say no and you survive it."
3. Name + next move: "Alex, open with the conclusion."

**Pitfalls** — A name with no content is just shouting. The Kross 2014 protocol had people reflect on their feelings using their own name for minutes, not chant it once. Evidence-backed, see core and Sources.

**Examples** — constructed example: in the car before a first date: "Jordan, you're nervous because you hate silences. You've got three questions ready. Start with the trip one."

### The 3 a.m. replay

**Context** — Lying awake, rerunning a daytime exchange: the sentence you fumbled, the other person's frown. Each loop adds feeling, not information.

**Reading** — (1) The brain's unfinished-business scan asking for one honest pass; (2) Immersed first-person replay, the definition of rumination; (3) Daytime suppression billing you at night.

**Response options**
1. Watch it once as footage of someone else, then stop the screening.
2. Get up and write 15 minutes: what happened, how you feel, one small step tomorrow. Evidence-backed single-session version of the Pennebaker protocol, see Sources.
3. Schedule it: "I'll think about this properly at 3 p.m." Most scheduled worries don't show up.

**Pitfalls** — Arguing with the replay in bed; you can win and still not sleep. "Don't think about it" requires thinking about it.

**Examples** — constructed example: after saying the wrong thing in a meeting, eight mental reruns; the note said "clarify one line tomorrow" and sleep came in ten minutes.

### "They'll think I'm stupid"

**Context** — Before speaking up in a meeting, posting in a group chat, presenting to seniors. Mind-reading aimed at an audience that hasn't reacted yet.

**Reading** — (1) Projection of your own harshest review onto the room; (2) Memory of one real past humiliation doing the talking; (3) Accurate pattern read, occasionally true in genuinely hostile rooms.

**Response options**
1. Name + evidence check: "Sam, name one person in that room who actually said something like that."
2. Cost symmetry: "If it lands badly, I lose five minutes. If I stay silent, I lose the point."
3. Shrink the ask: one sentence first, opinions later.

**Pitfalls** — Treating the forecast as data. Nobody's imagined audience has ever been surveyed.

**Examples** — constructed example: the question Sam finally asked was the one three other people later admitted they'd wanted answered.

### The 15-minute dump before a hard talk

**Context** — The evening before a negotiation, apology, or family conversation, when your head keeps rehearsing catastrophes instead of content.

**Reading** — (1) The nerves are real and need somewhere to go before the agenda can form; (2) Written rehearsal beats mental rehearsal because ink forces finish; (3) A risk of pre-writing a script so rigid that live responses throw you.

**Response options**
1. Pennebaker-style single session: 15 minutes on what this situation means and how you feel about it, then close the notebook. Evidence-backed, see Sources.
2. Two-column prep: what I need / what they likely need, then one opening sentence for each.
3. Name-based preflight: "Alex, what do you actually want from tomorrow?" spoken aloud once.

**Pitfalls** — Dumping the script into the conversation verbatim. The writing is for your state, not their lines.

**Examples** — constructed example: before asking for a raise, one written page ended with "I'm scared of seeming greedy"; saying the want plainly was easier after the fear had been read.

### Affirmations that feel fake

**Context** — Staring in the mirror at "I am confident and amazing" and hearing an internal objection in real time. Common when the gap between words and self-view is wide.

**Reading** — (1) The statement outruns your evidence, so the mind files an objection; (2) Wrong channel: you need a process sentence, not a verdict; (3) Fine as-is, just low-yield. The sources in this KB show community-level support for affirmations (anecdotal) and experimental support for distanced self-talk instead.

**Response options**
1. Swap verdicts for process: "I'm getting better at this" beats "I'm amazing."
2. Swap first person for your name: "Sam, you've prepared" recruits the observer.
3. Add a falsifiable fact: "I've run this deck twice without notes."

**Pitfalls** — Repeating "don't be nervous": the brain must process "nervous" before denying it. Give it a forward instruction instead.

**Examples** — constructed example: replacing "I'm great at interviews" with "Priya, slow down, answer the question asked" turned the mantra into an instruction she could actually follow.

### The 10-minute reflection window

**Context** — Distinguishing reflection from rumination in real time. Use when you catch yourself on loop three or more.

**Reading** — (1) Reflection has a deliverable: a next step; (2) Rumination has a soundtrack: the same sentence, louder; (3) Timed reflection can collapse into rumination if the timer lapses unnoticed.

**Response options**
1. Set 10 minutes, paper only, exit question fixed: "what's one thing I'd do differently?"
2. Distanced framing throughout: replay it once as an observer, not through your own eyes.
3. When the timer ends, write the one step and close. Loop resumes, restart the window tomorrow.

**Pitfalls** — Confusing depth with duration. Ten honest minutes beat two foggy hours; the research distinction is perspective and goal, not stamina.

**Examples** — constructed example: a project post-mortem done in ten timed minutes produced one retro item; the previous three-hour version produced three complaints and no item.

### "What would I tell a friend?"

**Context** — Self-criticism running for days after a failure. The Neff calibration: kindness over judgment, common humanity over isolation, mindfulness over exaggeration. Evidence-backed, see Sources.

**Reading** — (1) You hold a harsher standard for yourself than for anyone you like; (2) The isolation belief ("only me") is doing extra damage on top of the failure; (3) A genuine need for comfort, not a character flaw.

**Response options**
1. Friend-version rewrite: write the sentence you'd say to a friend, then say it to yourself verbatim.
2. Common humanity line: "Anyone doing this for the first time would botch it."
3. Kindness with hands: schedule one concrete act of care (food, sleep, a walk). Compassion lands in behavior.

**Pitfalls** — Using self-compassion as a permission slip to stop trying. Neff's own framing points the opposite way: it fuels a growth orientation. Evidence-backed, see core.

**Examples** — constructed example: after failing the driving test, the friend-version was "first attempt, of course it's rough; here's what the examiner marked"; said to himself, then booked the next slot that week.

### Keep one promise to yourself, out loud

**Context** — Chronic self-abandonment: resolutions made silently and dropped quietly. Intrapersonal communication as a credibility ledger. No experimental source in this KB, so this is practice advice.

**Reading** — (1) Spoken promises raise the internal cost of skipping; (2) Oversized promises stock the ledger with defaults and teach you to ignore yourself; (3) The point is identity evidence, not the task's size.

**Response options**
1. Minimum unit: "ten minutes, now," upgrade only after it's done.
2. Name + deadline, spoken: "Sam, email goes out by nine."
3. Confirm out loud on completion: "said, done." Credit the ledger.

**Pitfalls** — Stacking five big promises and defaulting on all five. You become your own unreliable narrator. Promise smaller than feels worth saying.

**Examples** — constructed example: a dormant gym membership became a spoken "seven a.m., ten-minute walk" for five days, then running, then the gym, by week two.

### Morning script

**Context** — The first hour sets the day's internal tone; most people spend it on their phone letting other voices go first. This is the cheapest slot for deliberate self-talk.

**Reading** — (1) A short spoken script beats passive scrolling as a first input; (2) Can decay into background noise if the words carry no tasks; (3) Works best paired with one physical anchor (coffee, shoes on).

**Response options**
1. Three lines, spoken: today's one priority, one worry named with your name, one small kindness to yourself.
2. Question form: "What would make today a win?" answered out loud.
3. Written variant: one line in the notebook, same time, same pen.

**Pitfalls** — Turning the script into a second to-do list and quitting in a week. The script manages state, the calendar manages tasks.

**Examples** — constructed example: "Dana, today is the pitch. The one thing that matters is the demo. If it wobbles, you've still got the appendix." Said while the kettle boiled.
