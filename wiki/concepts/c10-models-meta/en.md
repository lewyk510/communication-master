LANE — authored natively, NOT a translation

This lane collects high-signal English phrasings that expose which model is running underneath a conversation, or that call for a meta-level move. Mechanisms (transactional model, Transactional Analysis, SCARF, CAT, trust dimensions) live in [[concepts/c10-models-meta/core]].

### "Let me finish."

**Context** — mid-argument or a tense meeting; you tried to interject. Between equals it reads as a request; with a power gradient it reads as instruction.

**Reading** — 1) transactional awareness: the sender wants one uninterrupted transmission; 2) certainty threat: they fear being derailed midway; 3) Parent-state takeover ("listen to me" cadence).

**Response options**
1. Hold for a beat, let the full thought land (Adult-to-Adult, protects their autonomy).
2. If stakes are actively growing: "Go ahead, then I'll pick it up from my side." (announced turn-taking)
3. If one-way speech is the standing pattern: "Since we've started, I'd like my actual fact on the table before we close." (gentle fairness claim)

**Pitfalls** — Treating every interruption as rudeness. What's wanted may simply be full reception; interrupting in return hits certainty and fairness at once.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Let me finish — the deadline moved, so I need the signed form before Friday, not after."

### "Fine. Whatever you want."

**Context** — deciding where to eat, whether to change plans; surface-level concession with a loaded emotional layer.

**Reading** — 1) genuinely indifferent; 2) sulking Child: "my pick got overridden last time"; 3) Parent-style shutdown: exiting the negotiation (avoiding conflict style).

**Response options**
1. Replace the open question with a choice between two: "A or B?" (removes the certainty burden, returns to Adult)
2. Fish for the buried preference: "You mentioned that noodles place earlier — still on the table?"
3. If this is a standing pattern: discuss the pattern later, cold, not in the moment.

**Pitfalls** — Logging "fine" as agreement, then auditing compliance later. The real reservation got killed at the start.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Fine, whatever you want." — later that evening: "Last time I overrode your plan; this one's yours, I'll go with it."

### "That's not what I meant."

**Context** — you paraphrased their position and they immediately correct you. A live semantic-noise event.

**Reading** — 1) your decoding genuinely missed; 2) their encoding was vague, both sides at fault; 3) the position landed badly and they're re-encoding (softening).

**Response options**
1. Re-paraphrase more precisely: "So you mean A, with B still open — right?" (Adult-level checksum)
2. Ask the negative: "So this doesn't apply to X, correct?" (elimination is faster than guessing positively)
3. Third correction in one conversation: move the issue from "your meaning" to "let's fix how we encode this."

**Pitfalls** — Winning the debate with "but last week you said…"; old accuracy proves nothing about current meaning.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "I didn't mean weekend overtime — I meant we sequence the client piece first; the schedule itself doesn't move."

### "Do whatever you like, then."

**Context** — a conflict took one step up; someone pulls the exit lever. Sounds like concession, functions as a stop-loss.

**Reading** — 1) avoiding conflict style: reneging on the argument, not the relationship; 2) Child anger wrapped in Parent authority; 3) an honest withdrawal: "I can't carry this conversation further right now."

**Response options**
1. Accept the exit: "Okay, pausing here. I'll come back to the detail tomorrow morning." (keeps the channel open)
2. If the issue genuinely matters: "One request before we drop it: facts only; emotions after we've slept."
3. Name the meta-question: "You're drained, so let's stop. Same time tomorrow?"

**Pitfalls** — Chasing them back in. Reading withdrawal as "they're right" — it's usually conflict avoidance, not a verdict.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Do whatever you like, then." — reply: "Noted. Parking it. I'll drop you two options in the morning and you pick whichever."

### "I'm only saying this because I care about you."

**Context** — parent to adult child, lead to report, partner to partner; usually appended after advice.

**Reading** — 1) genuine benevolence; 2) Parent-state framing that caps your autonomy while asserting their authority; 3) face-saving after unaccepted advice.

**Response options**
1. Accept the goodwill, decline the mandate: "I hear the care. Final call is mine, and I'll tell you how it went."
2. If it's a control bid: "Thanks — but this one I need to try myself."
3. Surface the implied instruction: "So what would you have me do, exactly?" (moves the Parent agenda onto the Adult table)

**Pitfalls** — Reading every "because I care" as manipulation; equally, using the phrase as a shield against being questioned. Label benevolence separately from control.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "I'm only saying this because I care about you — don't co-sign that loan."

### "Understood?" / "Got it?"

**Context** — end of an instruction brief. Neutral in operations, charged in hierarchies.

**Reading** — 1) plain certainty management; 2) status play: "I speak, you confirm"; 3) the sender is the one confused, and transfers the verification burden onto you.

**Response options**
1. Paraphrase as deliverable: "Two things: A by Wednesday, B after your sign-off." (more informative than "yes")
2. Expose the gap: "Step C hasn't reached me — which template do you want?"
3. Add a downstream check: "I'll walk you through my version this afternoon."

**Pitfalls** — Snapping "what do you think?" back; in power-gradient settings that reads as a status challenge. Family use is usually habit, not dominance.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Got it?" — "Got the shape; two open points: the quote goes out today, logistics waits for your one-liner."

### A sudden switch to stiff, formal register

**Context** — someone who normally texts casually suddenly deploys full punctuation and formal phrasing.

**Reading** — 1) divergence (CAT): deliberate distance, marking non-alignment; 2) borrowed formality as armor while angry; 3) third-party awareness: this may be screenshotted, so S3 composure kicked in.

**Response options**
1. Read the content first — formal prose often carries the facts more accurately. Decouple temperature from content.
2. If it signals rupture: don't mirror the formality back (divergence meeting divergence deadlocks); answer with one warm, plain sentence.

**Pitfalls** — Reading formality as "they're done with me". In workplace channels (S3) it's just genre convention.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: after a spat, the person who always sends voice notes sends a fully punctuated paragraph. Translation: "I'm speaking seriously; receive it seriously" — not "goodbye".

### "Let's just meet halfway" / "peace on this"

**Context** — mediation at family dinners, team standoffs, relationship repair attempts.

**Reading** — 1) compromising in good faith: genuinely splitting the difference on facts; 2) accommodating pressure: "everyone" must yield, but really one party; 3) a device to close discussion that still needs closing.

**Response options**
1. Split emotion from facts: "Feelings-wise I'll soften; factually, the invoice total needs an actual number."
2. Make the fairness explicit: "I can move X; I need a move on Y in return."
3. If the difference is genuinely minimal: "Deal," and stop rehashing.

**Pitfalls** — "Meet halfway" in family settings usually means the younger party concedes more. Also: treating harmony as proof the substance no longer matters.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Let's just meet halfway on the deposit — you cover two-thirds, I cover the rest, and we drop the interest question."

### "I'm just a nobody" / "I'm terrible at this"

**Context** — self-deprecation at a drinks table, after a rejection, or in comparisons.

**Reading** — 1) social grease, not a verdict; 2) pre-emptive status reset: lower myself first so you can't; 3) a real depression signal if recurring and persistent.

**Response options**
1. Redirect to specifics: "The part that was hard was the handover, and you did that clean." (status via concrete evidence, supports benevolence)
2. Plainly disagree without inflating: "Not accurate, but I take the point you're tired."
3. If it recurs with mood/sleep changes: ask seriously, drop the humor.

**Pitfalls** — Over-cheering ("No! You're amazing!") which can dead-end the exchange. Self-deprecation is often a bid for a specific kind of acknowledgement.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "I'm just a nobody." — "Nobody ran the client call alone when the lead was out. That's you."
