High-signal English phrases and what they usually carry underneath. Each entry: context, candidate readings, response options, pitfalls, examples. Mechanisms (implicature, face, indirect speech acts) live in [[concepts/c1-subtext-implicature/core]].

### "We'll see"

**Context** — Parent to child, partner to partner, boss to report; mostly spoken, often after a request. Very common in dating apps too.

**Reading** — (1) Genuine deferral: a real decision gated on later conditions; (2) A soft no dressed as postponement, the default adult reading; (3) In dating contexts, a polite fade-out signal. Community sources (Reddit/Quora threads captured in raw search JSONs) overwhelmingly read it as (2)/(3) in dating — anecdotal, no controlled evidence.

**Response options**
1. Convert vagueness into a checkpoint: "Sure. I'll check back Thursday, or tell me what would make it a yes."
2. Accept the soft no gracefully: "No problem. If things change, you know where I am."
3. With kids, honor it literally once: treating a genuine "we'll see" as a no erodes trust the other way.

**Pitfalls** — Pushing for a firm answer on the spot usually converts (1) into (2). One follow-up is fine; two is pressure.

**Examples** — constructed example: "Can we get a dog?" / "We'll see." One week later the parent has said nothing. Reading (2) was correct; the asker who scheduled a calm follow-up with reasons, not nagging, sometimes flips it to (1).

### "It's cold in here"

**Context** — Living rooms, offices, shared cars. A declarative sentence doing the work of a request.

**Reading** — (1) A request to close the window or turn down the AC — the textbook indirect speech act (captured in the IU pragmatics materials); (2) Literal small talk; (3) An invitation to leave the room ("we should go").

**Response options**
1. Respond to the function, not the form: close the window. Replying "yes, it is" and doing nothing is the classic literalism fail.
2. If unsure, make the request explicit cheaply: "Want me to shut the window?"

**Pitfalls** — Answering the surface question ("Is it? I hadn't noticed") is technically true and socially tone-deaf; you just failed the most-studied implicature in pragmatics.

**Examples** — Evidence-backed: the EBSCO research-starter snippet captured in raw/ uses exactly this sentence for ambiguous indirect requests. constructed example: roommate says it while you both sit in the draft; you close the window without a word; both of you win.

### "Could you...?" vs "Can you...?"

**Context** — Requests in work email, retail, anywhere politeness is graded.

**Reading** — Both are requests, not questions about ability — conventionally-indirect (CI) strategies in the CCSARP continuum captured at IU. "Could you possibly...?" and "Would you mind...?" scale up the imposition-acknowledgment; "Can you...?" is the neutral register. The graded forms are negative politeness: they redress the threat to the hearer's negative face (freedom from imposition).

**Response options**
1. Answer the request: "Sure, sending it now."
2. If you cannot, refuse the act without refusing the person: "I can't today, but Thursday works."
3. When you need to be equally soft back: "Would it be OK if I got it to you Thursday instead?"

**Pitfalls** — Answering the literal question ("Yes, I could, but I won't") reads as hostile wit. Also, don't escalate forms for small asks; "Would you be so kind as to pass the salt" lands as sarcasm.

**Examples** — Evidence-backed: IU materials list "Can I borrow your notes?" vs "Can you loan me your notes?" as speaker- vs hearer-oriented request projections, chosen by age, social distance, and imposition level.

### "Good for you"

**Context** — Reaction to someone's announcement or achievement; tone does all the work.

**Reading** — (1) Genuine congratulation, warm tone; (2) Dismissiveness — "your news is not that big"; (3) Sarcasm, flat or clipped tone. China Daily's language column captures this split: the phrase is "superficially a blessing... actually carries a slightly mocking edge" in certain tones (tier 2 capture).

**Response options**
1. If it landed flat, test with one line: "That sounded less excited than I hoped. What's up?"
2. If you're the speaker and mean (1), upgrade specificity: "Good for you — genuinely, that promotion was earned."

**Pitfalls** — Using it reflexively in text, where tone is invisible, defaults you toward (2)/(3). In writing, replace it with concrete congratulations.

**Examples** — constructed example: "My boyfriend bought me a Gucci bag." / "Good for you." (the exact exchange pattern China Daily flags as sarcastic in tone).

### "As long as you're happy"

**Context** — Reaction to a plan or choice the speaker didn't propose and doesn't endorse; partners, parents, friends.

**Reading** — (1) Real blessing: I genuinely defer to you; (2) Resignation: I've stopped arguing; (3) Sarcasm: I disapprove and you'll hear about it later. China Daily gives "as long as you're happy" as the idiomatic English for the Chinese "你开心就好" family (tier 2 capture).

**Response options**
1. Probe once: "Happy-happy, or just done-arguing happy?"
2. If you're the speaker and mean (1), add the sincerity marker the Chinese usage also uses: "As long as you're happy — and I mean that."

**Pitfalls** — In text with no emoji, (2) is the modal reading. Don't take it as endorsement in writing.

**Examples** — constructed example: "I'm quitting to join a startup." / "As long as you're happy." Follow-up question determined it was (2) with real worries attached — exactly why the probe matters.

### "With all due respect..."

**Context** — Meetings, reviews, any disagreement with a superior or client.

**Reading** — Almost always (1): a disagreement is coming and the phrase is an apology lease. Some use it sincerely (2), but listeners hear the lease. It functions as negative politeness prep — acknowledging status before threatening the other's positive face.

**Response options**
1. As listener, take the content seriously and skip the offense: the phrase already paid the toll.
2. As speaker, consider the honest alternative: "I see it differently" — often lands better than the ritual opener.

**Pitfalls** — Stacking it ("with all due respect, with all due respect...") signals contempt. One use per disagreement.

**Examples** — constructed example: "With all due respect, that timeline assumes zero rework." The room heard a fight; the phrase kept it a disagreement instead of an insult.

### "Interesting."

**Context** — One-word written or spoken response to your idea, pitch, or story.

**Reading** — (1) Genuine interest (rare, usually with follow-up questions); (2) "I don't know what to do with this" polite parking; (3) Damning with faint praise — the canonical implicature family the Stanford Encyclopedia lists under things people implicate without saying (tier 1 capture).

**Response options**
1. Force a concrete next move: "Interesting how? What would make it a yes or a no?"
2. Read the meeting: if nobody builds on your idea after "interesting," treat it as (2)/(3) and regroup.

**Pitfalls** — Celebrating the "interesting" as approval. No follow-up questions = no interest.

**Examples** — constructed example: pitch ends; investor says "Interesting." Silence. The founder asks "Would you want this in the next data room?" — converting the parking ticket into a decision.

### "Let's keep in touch"

**Context** — End of networking events, conferences, exit interviews.

**Reading** — (1) Ritual exit line, meaning ~goodbye; (2) Genuine, signaled by the speaker pulling out their phone or naming a channel; (3) Keeping a door open for a specific future need.

**Response options**
1. Convert ritual to action within 24h: connect on LinkedIn with a one-line context reminder.
2. If you were the speaker and meant (1), don't promise specifics you won't keep.

**Pitfalls** — Waiting for them to reach out after ritual (1). The line transfers obligation to whoever cares more.

**Examples** — constructed example: two conference strangers exchange "let's keep in touch"; the one who sends a same-day note with a concrete question gets the relationship; the other gets a contact entry.

### "I'll think about it"

**Context** — Sales offers, fundraising asks, proposals, relationship ultimatums.

**Reading** — (1) Real deliberation with a decision horizon; (2) A no with the receipt kept: no is on file, face is intact (mechanism: off-record strategy, see core.md); (3) Shopping your offer elsewhere.

**Response options**
1. Attach a clock politely: "Of course. I'll follow up Tuesday — does that work?"
2. Surface the hidden variable: "Is there a part you'd change to make it easier to say yes?"
3. Accept and stop selling: continued pitching after "I'll think about it" reads as not trusting them to think.

**Pitfalls** — Treating it as a maybe-yes in your forecast. In Chinese business contexts, the equivalent "我考虑一下" skews even closer to no (see the zh lane).

**Examples** — constructed example: candidate says "I'll think about it" to an offer; recruiter books Tuesday follow-up; candidate needs the named date to avoid ghosting — everyone saves face.

### "No worries if not!"

**Context** — Email/Slack requests, usually with an ask the sender feels is burdensome.

**Reading** — (1) Real flexibility: the deadline is soft; (2) Face-washing a hard deadline — the "no worries" is ritual, the ask stands; (3) Pre-forgiveness for an anticipated no, sometimes genuinely wanted.

**Response options**
1. If you can't meet it, answer the actual choice, not the ritual: "I can't do today, Thursday works."
2. If you're the sender and (2), drop the tag: a fake escape hatch costs trust when people take it.

**Pitfalls** — Taking the escape hatch when the deadline was real (reading 2); sender annoyed, you confused. When stakes are high, ask directly: "Is that deadline movable?"

**Examples** — constructed example: "Can you review the deck by 5? No worries if not!" Intern takes the out. Deadline was real. The tag was negative politeness, not permission — a pure face-mechanics fail.
