This lane collects high-signal English sentences and meeting moments in team settings. Each entry gives context, candidate readings, response options, pitfalls, and an example. Mechanisms (psychological safety, groupthink, Tuckman stages) live in [[concepts/c15-group-team-communication/core]].

### "Any objections? ... Great, moving on."

**Context** — End of an agenda item in a meeting with mixed seniority; the leader allows two seconds of silence and closes.

**Reading** — (1) Genuine close: nobody really objected. (2) procedural shutdown: the question format raises the cost of dissent, and silence gets recorded as consent (matches Janis's illusions of unanimity: silence viewed as agreement, Evidence-backed in core). (3) Time pressure: the leader saw hands twitching and chose not to see them.

**Response options**
1. Convert silence into detail: "One concern before we close: what happens to X if the vendor slips a week?"
2. If it's too late, register dissent in writing right after: minutes, email, or the ticket.
3. If you chair, replace the broadcast question: "Let's hear one worry from each person."

**Pitfalls** — Silence is the most misread signal in meetings. Treat unanimous fast agreement on a contested topic as a finding, not a victory.

**Examples** — constructed example: "Any objections?" Nobody speaks. Three weeks later a teammate says, "I always thought that timeline was fake."

### The same two people fill the whole meeting

**Context** — A recurring meeting where two voices take most of the airtime; everyone else stays muted.

**Reading** — (1) Turn-taking breakdown: status or personality monopolizes the floor (see core on turn-taking and production blocking). (2) Topic mismatch: the quiet people don't see themselves in the agenda. (3) Quiet protest: disengagement aimed at the dominant pair.

**Response options**
1. Round-robin the room: "Before we decide, let's hear from the folks who haven't spoken."
2. Switch medium: silent doc-writing for five minutes, then read out. It bypasses the loud pair entirely.
3. If you're the dominant speaker, cut yourself in half: state your point, then ask one named person to react.

**Pitfalls** — Airtime distribution predicts decision quality better than headcount does. A quiet meeting is not an aligned meeting.

**Examples** — constructed example: 60-minute meeting, two speakers, 50 minutes of talk. After switching to silent doc-writing, nine previously unheard issues surfaced in ten minutes.

### "Let's take this offline."

**Context** — Mid-meeting, someone raises a hard question; the chair defers it "offline" to keep momentum.

**Reading** — (1) Legit deferral: real time constraint, will genuinely follow up. (2) Dissolution: the question is uncomfortable and "offline" is where questions go to die. (3) Scope control: the discussion threatens a decision already made.

**Response options**
1. Accept, but pin it: "Happy to. Who's in the offline group, and when does it report back?"
2. If it's your question and it's structural, insist once: "This affects today's decision, so can we take three minutes now?"
3. Watch the follow-through. An offline that never reconvenes is a (2).

**Pitfalls** — "Offline" without a named owner and a report-back date is a disposal chute for dissent. Count how many come back.

**Examples** — constructed example: "Let's take the budget question offline." Nothing lands. Next meeting the same budget passes unreviewed.

### Agreed in the room, argued in the corridor

**Context** — Minutes say "consensus", but the real debate continues in small chats and one-to-ones afterward.

**Reading** — (1) Self-censorship in the room: dissent existed but never surfaced (Janis's self-censorship symptom). (2) Missing consultation: an affected party was never formally Consulted (RACI mismatch, see core). (3) Political pre-meeting: the deal was already done before the meeting, and someone's performing now.

**Response options**
1. Write the decision back within the day and ask for objections on the record: "Confirming what we agreed: X, owner Y, deadline Z."
2. Talk to the corridor privately: "You had reservations. What would have made it safe to raise them in the room?"
3. Fix the process, not the person: pre-reads and anonymous pre-meeting input for the next round.

**Pitfalls** — Confronting them publicly with "you agreed!" teaches the team the wrong lesson: the meeting itself is unsafe, so stay silent next time too.

**Examples** — constructed example: minutes say "unanimous on Friday launch". The launch slips because ops raised its blocker in a private chat, never in the meeting.

### "Just playing devil's advocate, but..."

**Context** — A framing device people use before disagreeing, common in teams with uneven power or thin safety.

**Reading** — (1) Genuine borrowed license: they need the label to voice a real doubt. (2) Plausible deniability: an attack dressed as a game, they may not believe it but want the seed planted. (3) Test balloon: they're checking whether the room punishes this line of thought.

**Response options**
1. Honor the content, drop the costume: "Whatever we call it, that's a real risk. Let's put it on the list."
2. Watch what happens next. If their "advocacy" only ever targets one person or team, that's (2).
3. If it's your team's only channel for dissent, fix the channel: assigned devil's advocate roles or anonymous input.

**Pitfalls** — Don't mock the label ("no need for theatrics"). The costume is data: dissent here needs a disguise.

**Examples** — constructed example: "Just playing devil's advocate: what if the pilot data doesn't replicate?" The room exhales. Three others build on it within minutes.

### The silent video call

**Context** — Hybrid or remote meeting; the room talks, the remote tiles stay frozen on mute.

**Reading** — (1) Turn-taking failure: remote voices naturally come last, and interruptions kill them. (2) Information asymmetry: side conversations in the room carry content the tiles never hear (micro-mindguards, see core). (3) Learned futility: they tried once, got talked over, stopped trying.

**Response options**
1. Equalize the medium: everyone joins on their own device, one screen each, even people in the office.
2. Call on the farthest first: "Before we go deeper, Chen and Priya, you're on the client site. What are we missing?"
3. Shared doc as the parallel channel: remote folks type while the room talks; read the doc out loud before closing.

**Pitfalls** — "Remote people, feel free to jump in" is not an invitation, it's homework. Without structural turn-taking, they won't.

**Examples** — constructed example: office side chat decides the deadline "among ourselves". The two remote engineers hear about it from the commit log.

### Task dropped in #general, nobody claims it

**Context** — A request posted in a busy team channel with no owner, no deadline, and no named person.

**Reading** — (1) Diffusion of responsibility: addressed to everyone, so it's addressed to no one. (2) Unclear ask: the poster knows what they want but hasn't written it. (3) Cold resistance: someone objects to the task but won't say so in the channel.

**Response options**
1. Name one person and one deadline: "@Sam, can you take this by Thursday?" One name beats ten.
2. If it's your channel, set the norm: every request carries who, what, when, or it gets bounced.
3. If silence persists, take it private: channels are bad places to beg.

**Pitfalls** — Reposting with "@here" and exclamation marks accelerates the freeze. Nobody wants to be first to touch a hot potato in public.

**Examples** — constructed example: "Anyone have prod access?" Zero replies for a day. "@Dana, do you have it?" Three minutes.

### "We've always done it this way."

**Context** — Pushback against a proposed change, often from tenured members; can be calm or exasperated.

**Reading** — (1) Institutional memory: the last time this was tried, it failed for reasons worth hearing. (2) Rationalized inertia: Janis's rationalizing warnings, a groupthink symptom where counter-evidence gets explained away (Evidence-backed in core). (3) Identity threat: the old way is bound up with their expertise and status.

**Response options**
1. Mine the history first: "Tell me about the last time we changed it. What broke?"
2. Shrink the risk: "Agreed we don't flip everything. What's the smallest slice we could pilot?"
3. If (2), name the pattern gently: "We've heard the warning a few times. Can we write down what evidence would change your mind?"

**Pitfalls** — Dismissing the phrase as pure conservatism throws away the cheap failure data embedded in it. Teams repeat old disasters this way.

**Examples** — constructed example: "We've always done it this way" turns out to trace to a 2019 outage nobody documented. The new plan absorbs the guardrail; the change passes.

### Praise in public, corrections in... reverse

**Context** — A team where criticism lands in open channels and meetings, while credit travels quietly or not at all.

**Reading** — (1) Management habit: someone learned "hard on people" as a style. (2) Status display: public criticism of juniors signals rank. (3) Safety collapse: nobody believes raising a mistake will be met with curiosity (the Edmondson definition of psychological safety is being violated daily, Evidence-backed in core).

**Response options**
1. Flip the default for yourself: process corrections public ("we all missed this check"), behavior corrections private, praise public and named.
2. When corrected in public, convert it to a shared task: "Good catch on the data source. Shall we agree on where source-of-truth lives?"
3. As a bystander, don't pile on and don't freeze: restate the concern as a problem statement both sides can work on.

**Pitfalls** — If the person starts lawyering each point instead of reflecting, the channel is wrong. Move to private; the group audience has stopped helping.

**Examples** — constructed example: a lead corrects a junior's slide in front of the client. The junior stops volunteering slides; three teammates quietly reroute around the lead.

### Two owners, one decision, four meetings

**Context** — The same decision keeps being revisited because accountability is split or undefined; every meeting re-opens it.

**Reading** — (1) RACI failure: multiple Accountable parties means none (a matrix with two A's is a matrix with zero, see core). (2) Consensus theater: real disagreement was never resolved, only adjourned. (3) Power struggle: the decision is a proxy for territory.

**Response options**
1. Name the mechanism before the topic: "This one we decide today, by majority, and we log it."
2. Collapse to one A: get both parties' bosses in a fifteen-minute call, pick one owner, write it down.
3. If consensus is genuinely required, run it properly: written positions, objections on the table, and a defined acceptance bar.

**Pitfalls** — Re-deciding is more expensive than deciding wrong the first time on reversible calls. Reserve consensus for the irreversible ones.

**Examples** — constructed example: "ownership of onboarding" bounced between product and support for a quarter. One line in a RACI table ended the bounce.
