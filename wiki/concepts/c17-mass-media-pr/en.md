LANE — authored natively, NOT a translation

This lane collects high-signal phrases between journalists, organizations, and online publics in English-language media work. Each entry gives context, plausible readings, response options, pitfalls, and examples. Mechanisms (SCCT, 3R, framing) live in [[concepts/c17-mass-media-pr/core]].

### "We are aware of the reports and are looking into it."

**Context** — The first hours of a breaking story; reporters calling while facts are unverified. The classic holding statement.

**Reading** — (1) Standard holding line: acknowledged, nothing denied, nothing invented; (2) stalling if no follow-up ever comes; (3) legal drafting has frozen every other word out.

**Response options**
1. Always append a time anchor: "We expect to share an update by 5 p.m."
2. Funnel traffic: "Media inquiries: press@company.com."
3. Issue the staff version of the same line so employees don't freelance to the press.

**Pitfalls** — "Looking into it" with no update inside 24 hours reads as guilt. The holding statement buys time only if a promised update actually ships.

**Examples** — constructed example: A courier's warehouse fire video circulates at noon; the company posts one sentence plus "next update at 6 p.m." Evening coverage quotes the company once, neutrally, instead of quoting bystanders only.

### "No comment."

**Context** — A reporter asks a direct question on camera or by phone and the organization has nothing safe to say.

**Reading** — (1) Legally constrained; (2) hiding something; (3) we haven't done our homework. Journalists and audiences overwhelmingly read (2) or (3).

**Response options**
1. Replace with a bounded answer: "We can't discuss personnel matters, but here is what we can confirm..."
2. Say when you can talk: "We'll be in a position to comment after the audit closes."
3. If truly barred, say who barred you: "On legal advice, we can't comment during the investigation."

**Pitfalls** — "No comment" becomes the quote itself. The headline writes you as evasive even when you are simply constrained.

**Examples** — constructed example: Asked about a data leak, the CEO says "no comment"; the story runs under "Company silent on data leak." A peer company in the same position says "our investigation is ongoing, preliminary findings Friday" and gets "Company pledges answers."

### "We deeply regret..." vs "We apologize."

**Context** — Drafting the crisis statement; choosing between sympathy and responsibility (the Regret layer of the 3R structure; SCCT's diminish vs rebuild postures).

**Reading** — "We regret / are saddened" = empathy without liability. "We apologize / this was our fault" = acceptance of responsibility, asking forgiveness. Reporters parse the difference in real time.

**Response options**
1. Facts unclear: "We deeply regret the disruption and are establishing the facts."
2. Facts point at you: full apology plus remedy, no retreat to "regret."
3. False accusation: skip sympathy entirely, "the claim is false," deny posture.

**Pitfalls** — "Regret" where apology is owed reads as coldness; premature apology concedes liability. Calibrate against SCCT's responsibility clusters, not against your discomfort.

**Examples** — constructed example: A caterer's statement opens "we regret any inconvenience" after a food-safety closure; the local paper's pull-quote is the customers' "inconvenience?!" The corrected statement names the closure, the refund process, and the health inspection date.

### "That's off the record."

**Context** — Background briefings with trusted journalists; negotiating what may be printed.

**Reading** — (1) Ground rules agreed in advance: information usable but unattributed, or unusable entirely; (2) a trap when the journalist never accepted the term; (3) an attempt to plant a favorable frame while keeping deniability.

**Response options**
1. State the ground rule and get explicit acceptance before the sensitive line: "This part is background only, agreed?"
2. Prefer "on background / not for attribution" over full off-record if you want the fact to travel.
3. Never say it after you've said the thing; post-hoc off-record is widely treated as void.

**Pitfalls** — Off-record is a courtesy norm, not a contract; one sloppy reporter and the quote is public with your name on it. Never put in a briefing what you cannot survive in print.

**Examples** — constructed example: A PR lead briefs that a restructuring will touch 5% of staff "off the record, for planning context." Two days later a rival outlet runs "Sources say 5% cuts are coming." The quote carried no name and the company still wore the number.

### "Happy to connect you with our spokesperson."

**Context** — Media relations gatekeeping: who may speak, and routing the journalist to the authorized voice.

**Reading** — (1) Good discipline: one voice, one message, SCCT's consistency rule applied internally; (2) stonewalling when every request dies in the PR inbox; (3) genuine scheduling friction.

**Response options**
1. Offer a real slot with a real name within one business day.
2. If the spokesperson can't talk, send a quotable written line in their voice.
3. Prepare the spokesperson with the three hardest questions first, not the easy ones.

**Pitfalls** — All requests quietly unanswered turns "media relations" into "media avoidance"; the story then quotes your competitors and your critics. Silence fills the void with your worst transcript.

**Examples** — constructed example: Two firms face the same recall question. Firm A routes every call to an unresponsive inbox; Firm B answers within hours with its head of quality on record. The next day's coverage of A is entirely sourced from B's statements.

### "We hear you." (and other backlash non-apologies)

**Context** — Online backlash over pricing, ads, or an executive's remarks; the brand's first public post on X/Instagram/TikTok.

**Reading** — Audiences read "we hear you" as (1) an opening move toward change, or (2) a stub that acknowledges feelings while committing to nothing. If the next post is an unrelated product promo, reading (2) hardens into cynicism.

**Response options**
1. Pair it with one concrete commitment: "We hear you. The campaign comes down today."
2. Name the decision and the reviewer: "We've asked our head of brand to review every asset in the series."
3. If you disagree with the backlash, say so plainly instead of the empty empathy line.

**Pitfalls** — Empathy theater without remedy invites the follow-up post "they heard us and changed nothing," which doubles the original damage. Bolstering ("we've always supported...") is a supplement, not a substitute, for a real response.

**Examples** — constructed example: A bank's cringe ad is pulled within hours with one post: "We got this wrong. The ad is down, and here's what we'll do differently." Comment sections pivot from pile-on to closure; a rival that posts only "we value all feedback" stays in the cycle for days.

### "Let's take this to DM."

**Context** — Community management on public posts: a customer complaint is going viral in your comments.

**Reading** — (1) Legitimate escalation: private details needed, moving to DM is practical; (2) scrubbing the public thread of visible complaints; (3) copy-paste deflection.

**Response options**
1. Post one public, specific reply first, then move: "Sorry, three failed deliveries is not okay. DM your order number."
2. Return to the public thread once resolved and close the loop in public.
3. Volume spike? Switch from per-comment replies to one pinned statement.

**Pitfalls** — Public thread full of "check your DMs" with no visible resolution reads as a graveyard. The audience judges the fix by what they can see, not by what your inbox contains.

**Examples** — constructed example: An airline's comment section floods after a cancellation wave. Staff answer visibly with rebooking info and only go to DM for names and booking codes; the pinned post with the policy link outperforms any individual apology.

### "This post is sponsored."

**Context** — Influencer relations and paid content disclosure in English-language platforms (Instagram's Paid Partnership label, YouTube's paid-promotion checkbox, #ad).

**Reading** — Audiences accept sponsorship that is disclosed up front; undisclosed promotion, once exposed, destroys both the influencer's and the brand's credibility.

**Response options**
1. Put the disclosure where the audience actually is: in the opening line or platform label, not the caption's last hashtag.
2. Put the disclosure obligation in the influencer contract, with examples.
3. If a post surfaces without disclosure, add the label publicly and fix the process in the same post.

**Pitfalls** — Burying #ad among twenty hashtags technically discloses and practically deceives; regulators and audiences treat it the same way.

**Examples** — constructed example: A tech reviewer opens with "This video is sponsored, but the benchmark numbers are mine" and keeps full credibility; a peer caught taking money for "honest opinion" posts loses a measurable share of subscribers when the contract leaks.

### "For immediate release" / "Embargoed until..."

**Context** — The first line of an English press release; also a timing negotiation with journalists.

**Reading** — "For immediate release" = publish now; "Embargoed until [date/time]" = publish only at the stated moment, usually in exchange for early access. Breaking an embargo burns the outlet for future early looks.

**Response options**
1. Use embargo only for stories where lead time genuinely adds coverage quality (reports, launches with review units).
2. Keep the headline under ten words and the lead paragraph to the five Ws within 50 to 75 words (PRSA guidance).
3. Ship Tuesday-to-Thursday mornings in the recipient's time zone.

**Pitfalls** — Embargo plus a leak elsewhere kills trust in both directions; never embargo what a competitor can announce first.

**Examples** — constructed example: An NGO embargoes its deforestation report to fifteen outlets with review copies 48 hours ahead; eleven outlets publish at 00:01 on release day. The same NGO's earlier "immediate release" sent Friday evening got two pickups.

### "We've asked the outlet for a correction."

**Context** — Inaccurate mainstream coverage of the organization; media relations escalation.

**Reading** — (1) Routine correction request, supported by facts; (2) pressure campaign to soften fair criticism; (3) prelude to legal threats. Editors distinguish (1) from (2)/(3) by the precision of the factual objection.

**Response options**
1. Cite the exact sentence, the exact fact, and the exact source, in writing, to the editor.
2. Request correction, not deletion; corrections run with the story and are searchable.
3. Parallel-track a clarifying statement on owned channels with the primary sources linked.

**Pitfalls** — Threatening litigation over coverage that is merely unflattering converts a one-day story into a press-freedom story about you.

**Examples** — constructed example: A paper's story misstates a fine's amount; the company's letter quotes the court filing and asks for a correction, which appears within a day. Its earlier demand that a critical opinion piece be "taken down" had been refused and screenshotted.
