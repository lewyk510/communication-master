LANE — authored natively, NOT a translation

This lane collects English-language signals where gender, age or generation is doing work in the message. Mechanisms and the full evidence base live in [[concepts/c7-demographic-differences/core]]. Standing discipline for every entry: a demographic label may start a hypothesis; it never finishes one.

### "She's just more emotional than him"

**Context** — team debriefs, hiring chats, partner venting; often said with a knowing nod, as if it were settled science.

**Reading** — (1) A folk claim dressed as observation; (2) a genuine complaint about one incident, wearing a generalization as armor; (3) pretext: the label exists to justify a decision already made (skip her for lead).

**Response options**
1. Resize the claim: "That difference has never shown up as a reliable effect in meta-analyses. Which specific moment are we talking about?"
2. Offer the base rate: with hundreds of meta-analyzed effect sizes, communication-style gender differences land at d near or below 0.2, effectively unguessable for an individual.
3. Redirect to the person: "Let's decide based on how she actually runs projects, not the category."

**Pitfalls** — The manager who acts on "women are emotional" also manufactures the behavior: stereotype threat studies show labeling a task as gender-biased creates the gender gap it predicts. Label first, performance drop second.

**Examples** — constructed example: In a promotion round, one panelist says "engineering clients prefer a male point of contact." The useful move is asking for one concrete lost account where that was the reason. The usual answer: none can be named.

### He interrupted me again

**Context** — video standups, in-room meetings; you get cut off mid-sentence by a peer or manager, any gender either way.

**Reading** — (1) Dominance: worth noting only if he repeatedly cuts off a specific person; (2) overlap culture: an enthusiastic speaker racing the last syllable, not attacking (Tannen's high-involvement style); (3) eagerness to complete your thought because he believes he knows the ending. Meta-analytic gender effect on interruptions is d = 0.15, negligible; it flips by context (dyads vs groups, strangers vs friends).

**Response options**
1. Claim the turn in the moment: "Let me finish this point, then you go."
2. Calibrate privately after: "I notice I get cut off around you. Can we keep turns longer in these meetings?"
3. Watch three rounds before labeling: escalation to formal feedback deserves a repeated, person-specific pattern.

**Pitfalls** — The reflexive "mansplaining" verdict after one event overweights a d = 0.15 effect and tells everyone in the room that being accused is cheap. Keep attribution separate from behavior (see the competence-questioning research cited in [[concepts/c7-demographic-differences/core]]).

**Examples** — constructed example: A colleague jumps in on you twice; you also remember she overlaps everyone she is comfortable with, and treats clients formally. The pattern says habit, not slight.

### "Can you explain it to her? Girls never get the tech stuff"

**Context** — live meetings or side chats; someone routes a technical explanation away from a woman "for speed".

**Reading** — (1) Speed concern, unverified: the speaker never actually tested her grasp; (2) habit from a tool-savvy or tool-averse family script; (3) status move: re-legitimizing himself as the go-to expert.

**Response options**
1. Hand her the pen: "Actually she ran the last deployment, let her walk us through it."
2. Test skill, not cohort: everyone's digital fluency is an individual profile; digital-native myths have no supporting evidence.
3. Correct the frame softly: "Comfort with tech tracks exposure and training, not gender."

**Pitfalls** — "Digital native" thinking cuts both ways: assuming a Gen Z junior is fluent, or a Boomer manager is not, both fail against individual evidence. Suggest a quick skills check when routing matters.

**Examples** — constructed example: A customer-solution handoff goes to a colleague who never read the contract. Threshold question: who has actually deployed it. Answer makes the gender assumption irrelevant.

### "Gen Z just can't take feedback?"

**Context** — leadership workshops, hiring-review chats, recruiters' group threads; often echoes a consultant's line about a whole birth cohort.

**Reading** — (1) A real incident with a specific employee, generalized; (2) an echo of pop "generational character" content; (3) a disguised complaint that exit rates went up after a bad quarter.

**Response options**
1. Bring the evidence: the most comprehensive meta-analysis to date (143 independent samples, 158,000+ people) finds few systematic generational differences in values or attitudes; consultants keep selling the story anyway.
2. Demand a mechanism: "What exactly did he do when you gave the feedback? Which words landed badly?"
3. Point to the self-fulfilling loop: label a cohort unmotivated, mentor it less, and turnover confirms your prophecy.

**Pitfalls** — The National Academies (2020) report recommends against using generations as management categories; invoking cohort labels in HR can also create age-discrimination exposure, since workers 40 and over are heavily protected in most jurisdictions.

**Examples** — anecdotal + constructed example: A speaker claims "Gen Z needs praise three times a week." The honest question is "study?" There is famously no study; a comparable quote made headlines precisely as an example of folk wisdom, and a 2024 meta-analysis of the field's own papers shows how often such lines survive without data.

### "My daughter only talks to me through a screen"

**Context** — parent peers, family holidays, dinner with other parents; a complaint about frequency or depth of contact with a young adult child.

**Reading** — (1) Channel mismatch: her generation defaults to asynchronous messaging for logistics and voice for feelings, or the reverse for your aunt; (2) real distance she is managing; (3) the parent herself prefers phone calls and reads silence as rejection.

**Response options**
1. Match the channel to the ask: logistics by text, feelings by call or in person; announce the split explicitly to both sides.
2. Ask one open question weekly in her preferred channel; depth follows frequency.
3. Drop the nativeness frame entirely: her skill profile is hers, not her birth year's.

**Pitfalls** — "Digital native vs immigrant" explains nothing and predicts little; nineteenth-century equivalents ("that generation reads too many novels") made the same wrong bet.

**Examples** — constructed example: A father texts "call me more!" and gets silence; he switches to daily one-line texts and a standing Sunday call, and the month-end update call tripled in length. The channel changed before the closeness did.

### "Train the women, they're better at rapport; give the numbers to the men"

**Context** — client-allocation meetings, internal staffing chats; roles being assigned along split gender stereotypes.

**Reading** — (1) A shortcut meant kindly that still tracks a stereotype; (2) cover for favoring someone already in the inner circle; (3) genuine, memories of one successful past pairing, re-folded into a rule.

**Response options**
1. Name the cost of the pool: "Half our bench gets defined out of half our clients on a hunch."
2. Offer the small-effect evidence: rapport vs reporting style differences are small, context-dependent, and famously contested; individual track records exist for everyone on the bench.
3. Pilot it: assign on data for one quarter and compare client satisfaction by would-be stereotype lane.

**Pitfalls** — Both halves of the "compliment" box people in: the "natural empath" loses the analytical stretch, the "numbers guy" loses the relationship stretch. Tannen's popular framing has been criticized precisely for freezing people like this.

**Examples** — constructed example: A team tallies the last two years: women led three of the four most technical wins. The "girls do rapport" rule quietly dies in the meeting where the data appears.

### "OK Boomer" flying across the room

**Context** — intergenerational friction, often in text or open-plan banter after an older colleague dismisses a young one's concern.

**Reading** — (1) A dismissal returning fire: the younger speaker has decided persuasion is useless; (2) identity fun that can tip into contempt; (3) a request, from the person dismissed, to be taken seriously.

**Response options**
1. Restore specifics: "Which part of the policy did you find outdated? We can walk through it."
2. De-escalate the label: "Let's keep it about the decision, not the birth year; we've both been burned by that shortcut."
3. Privately coach the older party on the original dismissal that started the loop.

**Pitfalls** — Two cohorts stereotyping each other locks in the prediction: each treats the other as a fixed type, behaviors harden, both labels feel increasingly earned.

**Examples** — constructed example: A mid-career engineer waved off a junior's data-pipeline concern with "we did this before you were born." The junior replies "OK Boomer". A supervisor who asks only "what about your pipeline concern concerns you?" without the labels gets an answer in seconds.

### "He's from another generation, he can't change"

**Context** — HR mediations, family counseling, repeated conflict reviews; suggested at the end of an effortful failing cycle.

**Reading** — (1) A fatigue statement: the speaker wants permission to stop trying; (2) a label reading of one narrow trait as a total person; (3) a correct diagnosis that the fix lies in structure, not willpower.

**Response options**
1. Scope the trait instead of the cohort: he may never love emoji, yet could still answer invoicing questions the same way every day.
2. Shift to structural fixes: agreements, templates, escalation paths, which birth cohorts handle far more evenly than personality types.
3. If real change is needed from him: name the single behavior, the setting, and what both sides will do differently this week.

**Pitfalls** — Cohort-level talk also hides real age effects worth addressing (eyesight, hearing, energy); those are medical-and-ergonomic facts, not communication destiny.

**Examples** — constructed example: A father refuses chat messages but reliably answers anything written on paper left by the door. A daughter who changed the medium, not the man, ended a two-year standoff.
