# Public Speaking & Presentation — English phrases and in-room moves

This lane collects the spoken moves of English-language talks, pitches, and Q&A: what each phrase does to the room, and what you can run instead. Mechanisms live in [[concepts/c16-public-speaking/core]]. All exchanges are `constructed example` unless traced to a capture.

### "Today I'm going to show you three things."

**Context** — First thirty seconds of a talk, demo day, all-hands, or class; the attention maximum.

**Reading** — A promise plus an agenda. The number three does double duty: it caps cognitive load and it invites the audience to keep count, which keeps them tracking you. It also signals restraint: the speaker has already cut things.

**Response options**
1. "The first one is the cheapest, so we start there; the third one is the expensive one." — order the three and mark the payoff.
2. "If time runs short, I drop everything and keep number one." — promise a floor, which buys you permission to rush later.
3. "By the third one you'll know exactly what to do on Monday morning." — tie the agenda to a concrete payoff.

**Pitfalls** — Announcing three and delivering four is the fastest way to lose the room; the agenda is a contract.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "Today I'm going to show you three things: where the quarter went, why churn spiked in June, and the one change we ship next week."

### "The one sentence I need you to leave with is..."

**Context** — The closing slot, after Q&A, before thanks. Also usable as a mid-talk anchor.

**Reading** — A takeaway flag. The phrase tells the room that memory work starts now; listeners switch from processing to storing. It is the standard antidote to ending on an unscripted question.

**Response options**
1. "...and if you forget everything else, remember: cash beats margin this quarter." — takeaway with a ranking attached.
2. "That's the sentence. Now here's the action that goes with it." — pair the sentence with the call to action.
3. "Remember the warehouse from my opening? It's the same story, twelve months later." — reclaim the opening hook.

**Pitfalls** — Delivering the takeaway sentence twice in different words weakens both; say it once, verbatim, then stop talking.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "The one sentence I need you to leave with is this: we don't have a demand problem, we have a delivery problem."

### "That brings me to my second point."

**Context** — Between body sections, every three to five minutes; the ear has no scrollbar.

**Reading** — A signpost. It tells the audience where they are and resets attention for the next block. Plain but load-bearing: without it, three good sections blur into one long stretch.

**Response options**
1. "...which we've covered. Point two is where it gets uncomfortable." — signpost plus stakes.
2. "So that was the why; here comes the how." — signpost by contrast.
3. "Quick recap: point one, quality. Now, point two: speed." — verbal TOC refresh for large rooms.

**Pitfalls** — A signpost with nothing behind it ("moving on, moving on") becomes a tic; every signpost must gate real content.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "That's pricing. That brings me to my second point, and it's the one people argue about: the launch date."

### "I'm excited to be here."

**Context** — Backstage, in the corridor, or in the first sentence on stage; any pre-performance arousal spike.

**Reading** — Arousal reappraisal in action. Anxiety and excitement share one physiological state, so relabeling is cheaper than calming down; Brooks (2014) found speakers who said "I am excited" out loud performed better than those who tried to relax.

**Response options**
1. Say "I am excited" out loud, three times, before walking out. — the minimal intervention that survives contact with nerves.
2. "My hands are shaking, which means I care about this." — honest version for safe rooms; label it as caring, not fear.
3. Two minutes of brisk walking before going on. — spend the arousal so it doesn't leak into pacing.

**Pitfalls** — "Calm down" fails precisely because the state is high arousal; you cannot jump down two floors, but you can step across the corridor.

**Examples** — `constructed example` (backstage) "I'm not nervous, I'm excited. Same heartbeat, better story."

### "That's a fair question."

**Context** — Q&A, on receiving a sharp but legitimate challenge.

**Reading** — An acknowledgment move. It buys three seconds, converts an adversarial frame into a cooperative one, and marks you as someone who can take a hit without flinching.

**Response options**
1. "Fair question. Short answer: yes. Long answer: yes, but not this quarter." — compressed-first answering.
2. "Fair question, and honestly, it's where our plan is weakest." — concede a real point and keep your credibility budget.
3. "Fair question; the answer has two halves, known and unknown." — structure a complex reply on the fly.

**Pitfalls** — Using it for every question devalues the currency; and it must be followed by an actual answer, or it reads as a deflection rug.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "That's a fair question. I don't have the retention number for the EU segment in my head; I'll send it by Friday. What I can say now is the trend direction."

### "I don't have that number; I'll follow up by Friday."

**Context** — Q&A or a live review, when you are asked for a fact you don't hold.

**Reading** — An honest boundary plus a dated promise. Audiences forgive "I don't know" far more readily than an invented figure; the dated follow-up is what converts the admission into reliability.

**Response options**
1. "Not off the top of my head. Order of magnitude: about a third." — give the approximation, promise the precision.
2. "That sits with the data team; I'll carry the question over and close it by Friday." — named owner, named date.
3. "I won't guess on that one, and I'd encourage you not to trust anyone who does." — firm version for audit-flavored rooms.

**Pitfalls** — The promise is the whole move: a missed Friday costs more credibility than ten unknowns; never promise what you can't chase.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "I don't have that number; I'll follow up by Friday. What I do know: last year it was 17%, and this year won't be lower."

### "So what you're really asking is..."

**Context** — Hostile or muddled question in Q&A; the reframe before any rebuttal.

**Reading** — A restatement with three jobs: amplify the question for the back row, rewrite its wording into something answerable, and buy five seconds. If you restate it wrong, the asker corrects you, and the correction itself cools the exchange.

**Response options**
1. "So what you're really asking is whether we can hit June without new hires. Let me answer that directly: no, and here's the cheapest fix." — restate, answer, offer.
2. "If I hear you right, your concern is risk, not cost. Those need different answers." — split a bundled attack.
3. "Let me sharpen it: you want the date, not the method. Here's the date." — compress an open-ended challenge.

**Pitfalls** — A sarcastic restatement ("so you think none of us understand margin?") lights the fuse; restate generously, then check ("did I get that right?") before answering.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "So what you're really asking is: what happens to the pilot if the funding slips. Straight answer: the pilot continues, the scope doesn't."

### "Let's take that one offline."

**Context** — A repeat heckler, a personal vendetta, or a topic that would eat the whole session.

**Reading** — The parking move: give the person a visible landing spot while removing their topic from the main line. The room is already on your side, provided you answered the first challenge in good faith.

**Response options**
1. "That deserves more than two minutes, and I have twenty after this session. Find me." — a concrete offer beats a vague wave.
2. "We'd lose the other thirty people to that thread. Let's do it after." — say the quiet part out loud; the room nods.
3. "Let me hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet." — return the floor to the room; the heckler loses the spotlight without a fight.

**Pitfalls** — Parking the first challenge looks evasive; park on the second strike, not the first. Keep the tone light, the words are doing the work.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "Let's take that one offline. I'm serious about the twenty minutes; I'll be by the coffee. Right now, back to the data."

### "Let that number sit for a second."

**Context** — Right after a striking statistic or a hard conclusion; the deliberate pause.

**Reading** — A pacing move. The pause transfers the processing load to the audience and marks the statistic as heavy. It is also the speaker's own brake against adrenaline-driven speeding.

**Response options**
1. "Sit with that: forty percent. Not ten. Forty." — repeat the number with contrast to deepen the pause.
2. (Silence, three full seconds, then the next slide.) — the silent version; strongest in large rooms.
3. "You're probably doing the same math I did when I first saw it." — invite the audience to process out loud.

**Pitfalls** — A pause without a payoff number in front of it reads as lostness; write the pauses into the script like rests in a score.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "Churn fell by half. Let that number sit for a second. Half, in one quarter, from a single change to onboarding."

### "This slide makes one claim."

**Context** — Slide design discipline, assertion-evidence style; said while building the deck or while presenting a key slide.

**Reading** — The assertion-evidence contract: the headline is a full-sentence claim and the body shows only the evidence that proves it. Saying it aloud trains audiences to read your slides as arguments instead of bullets.

**Response options**
1. "The claim is in the headline; the chart is the proof. Nothing else on the slide." — declare the contract for the room.
2. "If the headline isn't a sentence, it's a label, and labels don't persuade." — the editing rule for your own deck.
3. "One claim per slide, so if you remember nothing else, you can reconstruct the argument from the titles alone." — the title-only test.

**Pitfalls** — Assertion headlines without real evidence become slogans; the evidence half is what separates this from shouting.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "This slide makes one claim: our pilot cut onboarding time by forty percent. The chart is the whole proof; the rest of the deck is the appendix."
