# NVC & Conflict Repair — English signal handbook

Written for English-medium conflict moments: workplace standoffs, couple arguments, group chats gone cold. Each entry stands on its own; this lane is not a translation of the zh or ms lanes.

### "You always / you never..."

**Context** — the classic escalation opener in intimate and family conflict. Usually appears after weeks of swallowed annoyance, in person or in a long late-night text.

**Reading**
1. A memory bias, not a fact: one recurring incident generalized into an absolute.
2. A proxy for a deeper unspoken complaint ("you don't care").
3. A signal the speaker has given up on incremental fixes and is now arguing the whole relationship.

**Response options**
1. Receive the emotion, skip the statistic: "Sounds like this has been building for a while. When did it start feeling like that?"
2. Shrink to one countable event: "Let's take just this week. What happened on Wednesday?"
3. If you are flooded, call a timeout with a restart time: "I can't hear this well right now. Give me 30 minutes and I'll come back."

**Pitfalls** — Defending with "that's not true, I don't ALWAYS..." starts an evidence war about the word "always" and never reaches the actual grievance.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "You never listen to me!" → "Okay, help me with one example from this week so I can actually fix it."

### "It's fine."

**Context** — flat-voice exit line after a disagreement, at work and at home. Often delivered while still visibly tense, or as a one-line text with a period.

**Reading**
1. Genuine acceptance: the issue truly doesn't matter to them.
2. False peace: it matters, but they've judged that saying so costs more than it gains.
3. Punitive withdrawal: it matters, and they want you to work it out yourself.

**Response options**
1. One specific probe, once: "I don't think it's fine, and that's okay. Was it the scheduling, or how it was decided?"
2. Accept the words and leave the door open: "Alright. If it changes, I'm around."
3. If flatness continues past a day, re-open with an observation: "You've been quiet in our last two catch-ups. Something I did?"

**Pitfalls** — Asking "are you SURE it's fine?" three times converts silence into an ultimatum. Taking the words at face value and permanently dropping the issue is the more expensive miss.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "It's fine. Do whatever you want." → rarely means either clause. Treat as reading 2 or 3.

### "Calm down."

**Context** — issued at a visibly upset person, usually mid-argument. Universally read as a ruling that the other person's emotion is illegitimate.

**Reading**
1. A sincere (if clumsy) attempt to de-escalate.
2. An avoider's exit wrapped as concern.
3. A power move: the speaker appoints themselves the referee of who may be upset.

**Response options**
1. Take the pause, refuse the verdict: "A pause works for me. Not because I need calming, but because this isn't productive. Back at 8:30."
2. Redirect to content: "My being calm or not isn't the issue. Can we look at the deadline question?"
3. If you said it and regret it: repair immediately. "That came out wrong. I do want to hear this. Go on."

**Pitfalls** — Nobody in recorded history has calmed down because someone told them to. The Gottman research angle: demanding composure mid-conflict blocks the other person's repair attempts from landing.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "Calm down!" "I am calm. The question is whether we're leaving at 6 or 8."

### "I'm sorry you feel that way."

**Context** — the corporate non-apology: in dispute emails, customer service calls, and break-up conversations. Grammatically an apology, structurally a dismissal.

**Reading**
1. Regret for your feelings, zero acknowledgment of responsibility for the act.
2. A deliberate lawyer-safe formulation to avoid liability.
3. A genuine but incompetent apology: the speaker doesn't know apologies have parts.

**Response options**
1. Name the missing part: "I hear regret. What I didn't hear is what you'll take responsibility for."
2. Supply the standard yourself if the relationship matters: "What would help is if you owned the missed handover, and we agree how it gets checked next sprint."
3. In low-stakes cases, bank it and move on; not every non-apology deserves a workshop.

**Pitfalls** — Matching a non-apology with your own counter-non-apology produces two people officially past it and secretly not.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "I'm sorry you feel that way." "I'd feel that way less if the report arrived on Friday like we agreed. Can you commit to that?"

### "Can we start over?"

**Context** — a classic repair attempt, usually made mid-argument by whoever notices first that things have gone off the rails. High success rate when received, catastrophic when rejected.

**Reading**
1. A genuine reset offer: "the last 10 minutes aren't the conversation I want."
2. A tactic to erase something they said (evaluate the ask, not the motive; either way you learn).
3. A bid for reassurance that the relationship survives the fight.

**Response options**
1. Accept and reset both directions: "Yes. I'll take back my tone; let's both start from the scheduling question."
2. Accept with one guardrail: "Yes, but the 6pm point stands. We just redo how we discuss it."
3. If you can't accept yet: name why and when. "Not yet, I'm too hot. Ten minutes and then yes."

**Pitfalls** — Rejecting a repair attempt ("don't think a do-over fixes this") is, per Gottman's research, exactly the move that predicts unhappy outcomes: repair attempts only work as a couple-level skill, and they must be both made and received.

**Examples** — `constructed example` Mid-text-argument: "wait, can we start over? I read your message in a worse tone than you wrote it."

### "Help me understand where you're coming from"

**Context** — an empathic opener for decoding an angry message or an odd decision by a colleague. Works in email and in person; tone matters more than words.

**Reading**
1. Honest curiosity, often the fastest route to the need behind the complaint.
2. A softer version of "justify yourself" — likely to be read that way if the relationship is tense.
3. Buying time to formulate a rebuttal (people can tell, and it reads worse than open disagreement).

**Response options** (when you are the recipient of this line)
1. Give the need, not the history: "I need the release to be boring and predictable. That's why I pushed back on the hotfix."
2. Test the sincerity: "Happy to. First tell me what I said that landed badly, so I don't repeat it."
3. If you don't trust it yet: answer in small installments and watch whether they reflect before rebutting.

**Pitfalls** — Saying the line and then interrupting after four seconds. The phrase only works with reflective listening attached: paraphrase what you heard before you respond to it.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "Help me understand where you're coming from on the budget cut." "Sure: I need at least one quarter of runway before I can commit to hiring."

### "I feel X when Y because I need Z."

**Context** — the I-statement template, the English workhorse of NVC practice: feedback conversations, roommate negotiations, co-parenting, standing up to a manager.

**Reading** — the template itself has one job: keeping the subject "I" so the listener's defenses stay down. It fails when the feeling slot contains a disguised accusation.

**Response options** (how to run the template well)
1. Fill all three slots with camera-visible content: "I feel discouraged when the draft comes back with three changes and no comment on why, because I need to understand the reasoning, not just the output."
2. Append a real request, small and checkable: "Would you be willing to add one line of rationale per change?"
3. If emotions are high, split it: state the feeling and need first, let them respond, and only then make the request.

**Pitfalls** — "I feel that you're being selfish" is a you-statement in an I-statement costume. Test: whatever follows "I feel" must be a word you could say to the person's face in front of others without it being an accusation.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "I feel anxious when plans change after 9pm, because I need some predictability to sleep. Could you text me by 8 if things shift?"

### "What would make this work for you?"

**Context** — the collaborative pivot (Thomas-Kilmann collaborating mode) in negotiations that have gone positional: salary talks, vendor disputes, dividing household labor.

**Reading**
1. A genuine invitation to move from positions to interests; the single most productive question in the TKI toolkit.
2. A stalling tactic while the speaker regroups.
3. A setup to price your concession ("so THAT'S what it takes" said with a wince).

**Response options** (when asked this)
1. Answer in needs, not positions: "I need to not be the on-call every weekend, not 'a 20% raise.'"
2. Reciprocate the question to keep it collaborative: "Here's mine... and what would make it work for you?"
3. If you don't know yet, say so honestly: "Let me think about that tonight rather than name a number I'll walk back."

**Pitfalls** — Answering with an ultimatum when handed a collaborative opening. Positions kill the pivot; interests feed it.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "What would make this work for you on the holiday shift?" "Covering my train fare and first pick of the New Year week. Then I'm fine doing it."

### Going quiet / stonewalling

**Context** — one party stops responding mid-conflict: left-on-read in a group chat, one-word answers at dinner, radio silence on a thread that needed a decision. The avoiding mode in its most social form.

**Reading**
1. Overwhelm, not malice: the person is flooded and cannot process (common in conflict-avoidant styles).
2. Punishment by withholding.
3. Silent disagreement: they've decided no, but won't say it out loud.

**Response options**
1. Send one closing message that requires nothing: "No rush on a reply. When you're ready, I'd like to hear your side."
2. Offer a channel with lower exposure: "Easier to talk voice than text? I can call at 9."
3. If silence persists past the deadline on a work matter, escalate the decision, not the emotion: "I'll proceed with plan A Friday unless I hear otherwise."

**Pitfalls** — @-ing them publicly ("why are you ignoring me?") turns avoidance into humiliation, the one thing that makes an avoider stay gone. Matching silence with silence gives the issue a free winter.

**Examples** — `constructed example` Two days of read-no-reply after a budget argument → option 1, then option 3 on the third day.

### "Let's agree to disagree"

**Context** — the premature closure line: drawn out of pocket when a real disagreement is 60% examined. Common in family politics and cross-department meetings.

**Reading**
1. Genuine respect: the speaker values the relationship over winning this point.
2. Fatigue: the argument has cost more than it's worth to them.
3. A shutdown of a conversation they were losing.

**Response options**
1. Accept and park it with a timestamp: "Fair. Let's revisit in a month with actual usage data."
2. Separate facts from values: "On the numbers I think we actually agree; the disagreement is what we prioritize. That part we can park."
3. Decline gently if the decision is imminent: "I hear that, but we have to decide by Friday, so I need to know which version you'd least hate."

**Pitfalls** — Using it to escape a conflict that will re-materialize as a worse one later. Agreeing to disagree about a decision that still has to be made is not closure, it's a deferral with interest.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "Let's agree to disagree on the framework." "Sure, but we still pick one by Friday. Which one can you live with?"

### "I appreciate you saying that."

**Context** — receiving a repair attempt, an apology, or a concession gracefully. The least-practiced half of the repair skill; Gottman's work stresses that repair must be both made and received.

**Reading**
1. Full acceptance of the olive branch.
2. Acceptance of the gesture with the issue still open: watch for a "but" behind it.
3. Politeness as a delay.

**Response options**
1. If you receive it fully, confirm and close the loop: "I appreciate you saying that too. Are we good on the Friday plan, then?"
2. If the issue remains, accept and re-open gently: "Thank you, that means a lot. Can we also fix the calendar so it doesn't recur?"
3. When someone accepts YOUR repair, let it land; don't re-litigate the original fight to prove you were right.

**Pitfalls** — Rejecting an offered repair because it was imperfect ("that's all well and good, but..."). A flawed repair received builds more trust than a perfect repair withheld.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "I appreciate you saying that. Honestly, I also could have flagged it earlier instead of stewing."
