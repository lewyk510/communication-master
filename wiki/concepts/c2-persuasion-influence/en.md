# Persuasion & Influence — decode and respond (English lane)

Signals an English-speaking counterpart uses when running persuasion plays, and how to respond without burning the relationship. Mechanisms and evidence live in [[concepts/c2-persuasion-influence/core]]. Constructed dialogues are labelled `constructed example`.

### "It's 20% off, ends tonight"

**Context** — retail, SaaS trials, marketplace carts; countdown pressure.

**Reading** — 1) genuine policy expiry (rare); 2) scarcity plus loss-aversion framing, where "tonight" is doing most of the work; 3) a probe to see if you'll pay for hesitation.

**Response options**
1. Demand the policy in writing: "Email me the terms; I'll sign tomorrow morning."
2. Accept the deadline, reverse the proof burden: "Fine, lock it in and send the contract now."
3. Walk-away test: "That doesn't work for me, keep my email."

**Pitfalls** — treating a countdown clock as facts; paying a premium to an artificial clock.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Flash price closes at midnight." / "Send the quote in writing and I'll reply tonight; no quote, no deal."

### "Here's a free sample, just for you"

**Context** — pitch meetings, booths, referral intros; reciprocity setup.

**Reading** — 1) honest goodwill; 2) a reciprocity trigger, the small gift primes a bigger yes; 3) a face trap where declining feels rude.

**Response options**
1. Thank explicitly and defuse: "Appreciate it, does it commit me to anything?"
2. Reciprocate symmetrically: "Great, then I owe you one candid question session."
3. Decline cleanly: "I can't accept what obligates me, do share the brochure."

**Pitfalls** — feeling owed a favor after an unsolicited gift; research says even unwanted gifts bind the recipient (core, reciprocation).

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Free audit for you." / "Nice, is it one audit or a plan to upsell. Which?"

### "Nine out of ten customers choose this plan"

**Context** — telecom, insurance, upgrades; social proof.

**Reading** — 1) true popularity; 2) a social proof claim with unverifiable base rate; 3) a fake testimonial play, common in Malaysian e-commerce scams (core, ethics entry).

**Response options**
1. Query the base rate: "Nine out of ten of whom? Show the cohort."
2. Move to review platforms: "I'll compare third-party ratings and come back."
3. Neutralize: "Popularity isn't my criteria, here's my exact use case."

**Pitfalls** — accepting "everyone" as evidence; testimonials are the cheapest thing to fake at industrial scale.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Everyone around here is switching." / "Everyone else has my sympathy and my rates differ; who are the nine?"

### "As the leading provider in Klang Valley..."

**Context** — B2B, contractor scopes, investment decks; authority signals on display.

**Reading** — 1) real reputation; 2) authority symbols standing in for evidence; 3) an authority plus social proof stack designed for your peripheral route.

**Response options**
1. Ask one caliber question: "What deliverable proves that standing, and for which client?"
2. Downshift the halo: "If a smaller firm said this, would you change your view? I won't change mine either."
3. Reframe to evidence: "Show me one support SLA and one penalty clause, that's the authority that matters."

**Pitfalls** — confusing credentials with argument quality; forgetting that the Milgram-class literature says symbols alone move behavior.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "The dean himself endorses it." / "Great, where does he sign off, and which textbook value has he shown me from?"

### "Bosses like you keep things simple, right?"

**Context** — high ticket services, property agents; liking plus flattery.

**Reading** — 1) honest rapport; 2) the liking lever, compliment is a Cialdini-class factor; 3) pre-framing you as a decisiveness-biased buyer.

**Response options**
1. Catch it out loud: "I'll take that compliment but assume a deep-processor mood today, take me slow."
2. Redirect to substance: "Simplify, yes, after we see through line items."
3. Rebalance: "Actually my approval includes a second reader, we'll both read this."

**Pitfalls** — letting flattery lower your processing mode, ELM says peripheral route gains die fast.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Bosses like you don't need to micromanage line items." / "Bosses like me first run a spell-check on line four."

### "We could start with something small..." and it escalates

**Context** — volunteering drives, incremental scopes, extended ops tasks.

**Reading** — 1) foot-in-the-door: small commitment first, big request later; 2) honest delegation; 3) your prior say-yes now enforcing consistency pressure on you.

**Response options**
1. State an escalation ladder upfront: "Scope me max to X hours and let's write it."
2. Name the pattern politely: "If we do this small request now, what's the next one?"
3. Partial help: "I can do the review but not the build, apply it now."

**Pitfalls** — consistency bias making you honor an implicit chain; meta-analyses show small effects only (r about .17), don't grant it extra scale.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Just review the deck, no ownership." / "Review takes 20 minutes, the deck usually becomes a build. Cap it at review and I'm in."

### "Total retainer would be huge... but let's say this smaller figure?"

**Context** — agency retainers, event planners; big ask then concession.

**Reading** — 1) door-in-the-face, hoping you reciprocate their concession; 2) real tiered pricing; 3) social capital play, accepting a hard-earned concession you now feel awkward to refuse.

**Response options**
1. Restate your top ask: "Large quote noted, keep the scope I originally described at my budget."
2. Evaluate concession structurally: "Which deliverables get dropped at the lower tier?" — evaluate value per ringgit, not guilt.
3. Trade in-kind: "I'll take tier B if it stays at liquidated pricing for a full-year."

**Pitfalls** — feeling that their concession obligates yours; the effect is real but small, and dies with a time delay between requests.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Full scope 5k, or 900 for basic?" / "Basic includes replacing the maintenance tier entirely, spell that out for me."

### "Usually it's RM8,000..."

**Context** — quotations, salary talks, freelancer day-rates; anchoring.

**Reading** — 1) an honest list-price anchor; 2) a made-up high anchor to set your reference frame; 3) an attempt to skip market calibration.

**Response options**
1. Counter-anchor with verifiable data: "Market listings say 4 to 6, I'll use 5."
2. Reset the unit: "Let's price per deliverable, not per retainer."
3. Show your range once: "My bracket is 5 to 6, walk me to a fact under it."

**Pitfalls** — negotiating against the given anchor as if it were your number; anchoring even works under deliberation when it is well-sourced, so bring better sources.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Peers pay 8k." / "Show me two peers. Meanwhile here's a market page, and it says otherwise."

### "How am I supposed to do that?"

**Context** — delivered deadpan by counterparties; calibrated question (Voss).

**Reading** — 1) a genuine constraint; 2) a Voss signature, they push the feasibility burden back to you without saying no; 3) a tone probe testing whether you'll lash out.

**Response options**
1. Answer it concretely with numbers: "Here are two ways I could do that: one, we postpone; two, we split the deliverable."
2. Mirror the one who owes the next move: "That's my question for the resource plan, who owns resource calls?"
3. Go calm and label: "Sounds like the timing seems impossible from where you sit."

**Pitfalls** — mistaking it as refusal and abandoning; answering with resentment instead of a plan loses the frame (Black Swan capture, tier 2).

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "How am I supposed to hit March with these specs?" / "Two ways: a rush review cycle, or a March-only core release. Pick one with me."

### "Let's not ruin this over details"

**Context** — contracts, family-in-business mixing, supplier disputes; unity pressure.

**Reading** — 1) sensible relationship repair; 2) a unity appeal used to skip diligence; 3) a feeling of "belonging" crafted to make you under-document.

**Response options**
1. Separate identity from terms: "Details are why we'll still talk in ten years."
2. Credit the bond in feelings, price the work in documents, never bundle the two.
3. Timebox: "Detail review today at 4pm, one hour, then we're done arguing."

**Pitfalls** — accepting "unity" as a reason to skip writing things down; Unity was added by Cialdini to move people faster with one phrase, use the power deliberately on both sides.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "We're friends; details later." / "Friends keep details, strangers keep ambiguity. Detail session scheduled at 4pm."
