# Propaganda & Fallacies — decoding in English conversation, feeds, and group chats

Signals and replies for fallacies and persuasion devices as they show up in English: arguments, ads, forwarding chains, AI answer engines, and office debates. Mechanisms live in [[concepts/c18-propaganda-fallacies/core]]. All dialogues are `constructed example`. The same framework applies to any political side; this page takes no side.

### Straw man (the misquoted position)

**Context** — second round of any argument; town halls, comment threads, family discussions. Most visible when someone paraphrases you with extra words like "all" or "always".

**Reading** — 1) Deliberate weakening of your claim because the real one is harder to beat; 2) An honest but sloppy summary; 3) A third party retold your point badly and it stuck.

**Response options**
1. Restate the exact claim with its boundaries: "I said X for this case, not 'everyone, always'. Let's lock that sentence in and continue."
2. Steelman and get a nod: "If I put it your way, you're arguing that... is that right? (wait for yes) Then I'll answer that version."
3. If the temperature is high, park it: "My original wording is right there in the thread. Sleep on it, we can pick this up tomorrow."

**Pitfalls** — Saying only "that's a straw man" without offering a version to agree on lets the distorted version keep copying itself.

**Examples** — "You want to defund parks entirely." / "No, I want to cut the landscaping line by 10%." `constructed example`

### Whataboutism and "look who's talking" (tu quoque)

**Context** — Deflection mid-critique; politically, it's a sport played by every side; in friendships it shows up as pointing at your own past.

**Reading** — 1) Refusing to defend the actual charge by opening a new front about the critic; 2) A genuine fairness point ("why single me out?") mixed in with deflection; 3) Joking self-defense, judge by tone.

**Response options**
1. Separate deed from argument: "Maybe I don't practise this perfectly. On its own, is this advice correct?"
2. Grant the fairness point once, then return: "Fair, others did it too. The question is still what WE do next."
3. Call-in later: "That line about my record didn't change the actual issue we were discussing, can we revisit it?"

**Pitfalls** — Digging up their past in return makes you the mirror image; also don't ignore the fairness point, it can be real.

**Examples** — "So the report is late?" / "Well, you submitted one late last quarter too." `constructed example`

### False dilemma (either/or on purpose)

**Context** — Urgent memos, sales framing ("buy now or lose out"), headlines, office ultimatums.

**Reading** — 1) Deliberate compression of options to force a fast decision; 2) The speaker can't see the middle paths either; 3) Rare cases where two options truly are mutually exclusive (legal or contractual) that need checking item by item.

**Response options**
1. List option C out loud: "There's a third path, pilot it for six months."
2. Ask what makes them mutually exclusive: "Why can't we do both at a smaller scale?"
3. Surface the buried premise: "Your question assumes a 'do nothing and we fail' default, let's test that assumption first."

**Pitfalls** — Rushing to choose inside the offered frame is the real trap; the deadline is often part of the manipulation.

**Examples** — "Sign today or your spot goes to the waitlist." `constructed example`

### Slippery slope (the dramatized chain)

**Context** — Parental warnings, policy objections, "if we allow this then next..." posts.

**Reading** — 1) Genuine fear projected onto the worst case; 2) An engineered anxiety to make you comply; 3) Sometimes a decent inductive chain whose probabilities were quietly overstated.

**Response options**
1. Press one link: "That step, does it happen 'almost always' or 'now and then'?"
2. Trade the chain for a tripwire: "Instead of fearing step five, let's set one red light now, and stop if we hit it."
3. With elders, soften the pace: "Okay, let's do step one your way and review in a week."

**Pitfalls** — The chain is only as strong as its weakest link; arguing against the dramatic last step is wasted breath.

**Examples** — "Give kids espresso and you'll end up with addicts." `constructed example`

### Appeal to authority (title outside its lane)

**Context** — Ads (the film star recommending watches, per IEP's classic case), "experts say" posts, consultants citing one study.

**Reading** — 1) Authority in the wrong subject; 2) Right subject but misquoted or cherry-picked; 3) Real expert consensus, which deserves actual weight, the boundary IEP draws.

**Response options**
1. Ask the field: "Respected in what, exactly? Is this that field?"
2. Ask for the primary source: "Any link to the study itself? I'd rather read the paper than the summary."
3. Accept consensus properly: "If several independent researchers agree, say so. Was it one voice or the field?"

**Pitfalls** — Anti-authority reflexes are their own bias; expert consensus is evidence, one loud professor is not.

**Examples** — "A famous investor said this beauty supplement works." `constructed example`

### Bandwagon ("everyone's doing it")

**Context** — Viral trends, comment pile-ons, office pitches claiming momentum.

**Reading** — 1) Popularity treated as proof; 2) Real enthusiasm but unverified claims riding along; 3) Possibly coordinated amplification, which is a different problem than being wrong.

**Response options**
1. Ask for one independently checked reason: "Besides popularity, name one reason you verified yourself."
2. Distinguish genuine consensus: "If this is the shared conclusion of people who each checked separately, that's different. Which is it?"
3. De-escalate with curiosity rather than combat: "Interesting, what made it take off so fast?"

**Pitfalls** — Flipping "majorities can be wrong" back at them wins nothing; the useful question is where the crowd's agreement came from.

**Examples** — "Twelve million views can't be wrong." `constructed example`

### Circular reasoning (the loop that goes nowhere)

**Context** — Customer service scripts ("the fee exists because the policy says so"), legalistic exchanges, deadlock after three rounds of paraphrases.

**Reading** — 1) The premise contains the conclusion, as IEP describes; 2) The speaker only memorized the script and has no other reason; 3) A large informative loop (like dictionary definitions) that isn't fallacious at all.

**Response options**
1. Put the halves side by side: "You're arguing A because of B, but B because of A. Anything outside that pair?"
2. Ask what grounds the script: "If a written policy covers this, which document?"
3. Escalate when the loop refuses to break: log the exchange and raise it to whoever owns the document.

**Pitfalls** — Treating a script-reading employee as a sophist is unfair; your move is the document, not the person.

**Examples** — "This version of the app is best, it's the one users like best." `constructed example`

### Appeal to ignorance ("no proof means")

**Context** — Rumor triage, "it's never been proven harmful", "nothing in the papers means they hushed it up".

**Reading** — 1) Absence of disproof taken as proof, one of IEP's two forms; 2) Absence of news taken as suppressed news; 3) Its mirror form, absence of proof meaning conspiracy, is the same fallacy running backwards.

**Response options**
1. Assign the burden: "Who's claiming what? The claim-maker carries the proof, that's the default."
2. Set a verify-by date: "Let's check one reliable source now, and if it's still unclear by Friday, we drop it."
3. Neutral resting phrase: "No confirmed verdict yet" beats both "false" and "cover-up".

**Pitfalls** — Declaring a verdict yourself puts you in a fact-checker role you usually have no evidence base for. Hedged phrasing travels better and ages better.

**Examples** — "You can't prove the product isn't contaminated." `constructed example`

### SIFT in daily use (lateral reading as a habit)

**Context** — Community groups splitting over a clipped screenshot; you share one chat with both a nurse and a conspiracy-prone uncle; forwardable outrage everywhere.

**Reading** — 1) Anger arriving before sources, a sign to stop; 2) Shared posts stripped of context, the normal case, as Caulfield argues context needs rebuilding; 3) A habitual DM chain that picks content by outrage, not accuracy.

**Response options**
1. Use SIFT yourself, out loud where useful: "I pause, then check the source, then get a full coverage view, then trace the original quote." Stop, Investigate, Find, Trace is the map.
2. Deliver lateral reading as a gift, not a lecture: "Unrelated, but I found more completeness from a non-viral page, here's the link."
3. Decline forwarding professionally: "I'm on pause for this one until I see the source name."

**Pitfalls** — Taking one article's word as fact is the single most common failure; SIFT's value is in not deep-reading the first thing you meet.

**Examples** — "I'd fact-check that clip before retweeting, or I'm out." `constructed example`

### Call-in and call-out (first move of the two)

**Context** — The same correction, two delivery modes; community threads vs one-to-one room, mentor or intimidating boss.

**Reading** — 1) Call-out in public: naming the move and the fallacy for a room of onlookers; 2) Call-in in private: same point, one-to-one, keeping the door open; 3) Both commonly start with a steelman, because a corrected person rarely hears the delivery underneath the first sentences.

**Response options**
1. Steelman first: "Let me say your case back to make sure I have it..."
2. Ask for the connector: "Between those two lines, what's doing the connecting work?"
3. Hand evidence with a face-saver: "Any of us could have been misled by that chart; here's the original data source."

**Pitfalls** — Naming the fallacy out loud ("that's a slippery slope") reads as a flex; the tag alone rarely changes minds, it mostly triggers defense.

**Examples** — "Between us, that wording on Q1 was a slip of mine, not policy. Let me redo it." `constructed example`
