LANE — authored natively, NOT a translation

### "No worries if not!"

**Context** — Email/Slack requests ending with a soft opt-out; colleagues, freelancers, and
vendors attach it to real asks constantly.

**Reading**
1. Genuine opt-out — the ask is priced as optional.
2. Opt-out for appearance; a flat no surprises them.
3. The sender is avoiding accountability for the ask.

**Response options**
1. Refuse-plain (S4): "I can't take this one on this sprint — Wil can, if it's urgent."
2. Negotiate (S10): "I can do a first draft Friday, but not the full build-out."
3. Comply (S9): if yes, answer without conditions: "On it — draft by Friday."

**Pitfalls** — Matching their softness with vagueness of your own ("no worries!" when you DO
worry) is the passive-aggressive pattern the captured style guides warn about: say yes when
you mean no. `Evidence-backed` (Mayo Clinic capture, passive-aggressive section).

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Can you review the deck by 9am? No worries if not!" →
"I can give you comments on slides 1–5 tomorrow; a full pass needs until Thursday."

### "Per my last email…"

**Context** — Corporate email threads; the polite-dig escalation marker when a reply was
ignored.

**Reading**
1. "You ignored me, and I am recording that."
2. Innocent re-ping in heavy inboxes.
3. Pre-escalation: CC of a supervisor comes next.

**Response options**
1. Reset (S6): re-answer the question concretely on one line — never defend the record first.
2. De-personalise (S9): "Answering inline below — flagged green."
3. Upshift (S3): if a third ignore, offer a 15-min call; send in writing what the call decides.

**Pitfalls** — Replying to the dig instead of the question turns procedure into conflict;
escalation ladder logic overlaps with the complaint engine ([[playbooks/p3-formal-writing/core]]).

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Per my last email, did legal sign off?" → "Yes — legal
signed off Tuesday, 14:02; confirmation attached below."

### "Do you have a quick second?"

**Context** — In-person or Teams ping, usually from above or sideways; "quick" is doing
unadmitted work.

**Reading**
1. Genuinely quick (one question).
2. 45 minutes in disguise — the classic floor-by-desk open.
3. Preface to bad news.

**Response options**
1. Scope-check (S6): "Sure — I'm free until 2:50; if it's longer, let's book a slot."
2. Postpone (S5): "Tied up until 3pm — 3:15 work?"
3. Comply (S9): if truly free now, say "Yes — walk me through it."

**Pitfalls** — Saying yes reflexively and visibly grinding through the meeting teaches people
that your yes is worthless; the boundary-set move (S3) keeps both sides honest. `Practice heuristic`.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: manager: "Quick sec?" → "Sure, until 2:50 — if it's the
H1 review that's a half hour, I've got 4pm today."

### "Let's circle back."

**Context** — Meetings and email threads; the universal deferral line in low-context corporate
English.

**Reading**
1. Real deferral with a deadline they hold.
2. A soft burial — no one will circle back.
3. Politics: the decision moved off-interlocutors to a quieter room.

**Response options**
1. Anchor it (S6): "Circling back on what — the date or the budget?" then "I'll propose a time
   by Thursday."
2. Take the pen (S5/S9): "You'll have a proposal in your inbox Monday 9am."
3. Silence-check (S8): if two deferrals, ask directly whether the item is parked.

**Pitfalls** — Accepting the fog and re-raising timidly three times; you become the only person
with a memory, and that reads as nagging. `Practice heuristic`.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Let's circle back on comp." → "Sure — I'll send two
comp options Wednesday so we can pick one on Friday's call."

### "With respect, …"

**Context** — British/international workplace English, esp. in writing; disagreement arrives
wrapped in courtesy.

**Reading**
1. Genuine courteous disagreement — rare.
2. The courtesy is the warning label: strong disagreement follows.
3. Copy-paste hedges from a template, meaning little.

**Response options**
1. Engage the content (S6): restate their strongest point in one sentence, then answer it.
2. De-escalate (S1): "I think we agree on the goal and differ on the route — let's compare routes."
3. Negotiate (S10): if their route has a real risk you can remove, trade for it.

**Pitfalls** — Mirroring the hedge ("with respect, I politely disagree…") doubles the fog;
answering hedged language with plain language loses nothing on these channels. `Practice heuristic`.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "With respect, this timeline is unrealistic." → "You're
right that it's tight. If we drop the audit step to v2, I can hold the date."

### "Just being honest! / Just joking!"

**Context** — Group chat or in-person after a dig; the retract-after-offence pair common in
Anglo casual settings.

**Reading**
1. Real joke, you over-read it.
2. Wound delivered, exit ramp prepared in advance.
3. Probe of what you'll tolerate.

**Response options**
1. Laugh and move (S7): genuinely fine — "Ha, fair — you owe me a coffee for that one."
2. Name it lightly (S3): "Funny once. Coming from you, that one stung — don't repeat it."
3. Quiet boundary (S8): no reaction now, shorter rope next time.

**Pitfalls** — Over-laughing teaches the group your discomfort line; the practitioner literature
on assertive styles points at exactly this repair cost (Princeton UMatter capture: passive style
lets others set your limits). `Evidence-backed`.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Relax, I'm just saying you've put on weight!" →
"Bodies aren't my punching bag or yours. Topic closed — how was the trip?"

### "Can we do it my way just this once?"

**Context** — Couples, flatmates, teammates negotiating an exception; rapport is real but the
precedent matters.

**Reading**
1. Genuine one-off, will not recur.
2. Foot in the door — one exception becomes the custom.
3. Test of who wins defaults.

**Response options**
1. Grant-with-clock (S10): "Yes, this once — and in January we review how it went."
2. Refuse-warm (S4): "No, because once it becomes a pattern I resent it — let's find a different night."
3. Probe (S6): "What's blocking you from the usual plan this time?"

**Pitfalls** — The second yes costs more than the first; recurring-exception drift is the
boundary failure mode the assertiveness sources single out. `Evidence-backed` (Mayo capture:
saying yes when you want no builds resentment).

**Examples** — `constructed example`: flatmate: "Can we do dishes my way just this once?" →
"Once, yes. From next week we split as agreed, or we hire a cleaner."

### "Idk lol" / "😂😂"

**Context** — Texting; ambiguity plus laughter as shield; common among younger senders on any
topic of medium intimacy.

**Reading**
1. Truly undecided, laughing at their own indecision.
2. Signal-out — dodged the question and is watching whether you'll push.
3. Buying time to think.

**Response options**
1. Pin the fork (S6): "So — Saturday yes or no?"

2. Match-and-relax (S7/S9): respond light, no follow-up demand.
3. Time-box (S5): "Cool, pick by Friday and I'll book."

**Pitfalls** — Treating laughter as evasive when it is just register; and reading register as
flakiness when it is a boundary — the fix is the fork question, not an accusation. `Practice heuristic`.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "idk lol about the trip" → "😂 ok — Friday deadline, one word: in or out."

### Passive-aggressive note: "Sure. Fine."

**Context** — Chat or in person after a no from you; clipped agreement with a flat period.

**Reading**
1. Still hurt, cooling down.
2. Agreed under protest — expect silent non-compliance.
3. Weapons-grade withdrawal; watch the next two weeks, not the next two minutes.

**Response options**
1. Verify (S6): "That sounded thin — tell me the part you're least happy with."
2. Trade (S10): "If this way works poorly, we change it at Sunday dinner — agreed?"
3. Let it settle (S8): no chase; re-open tomorrow with warmth.

**Pitfalls** — Accepting the flat "fine" as closure is the classic misread; flat agreement to a
real ask usually converts to future sabotage. `Practice heuristic`, consistent with the
passive-aggressive profile in captured sources.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Sure. Fine." → "I'd rather have a hard no than a flat
fine — what's the block?"
