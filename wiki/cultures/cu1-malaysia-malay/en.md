Entries for English speakers working or living alongside Malaysian Malays. Malay phrases appear as quoted signals.

### The soft "no"

**Context** — You make a request and the reply is "I will try", "I'll see what I can do", or a vague "okay, okay". Common at work and with vendors.

**Reading** — 1) Genuine effort without a guarantee; 2) a face-saving no; 3) the person needs to clear it with someone else first.

**Response options**
1. Accept gracefully and set a checkpoint: "No rush, can I check back with you Thursday?"
2. Reduce the ask so a yes is easier.
3. Quietly line up a fallback if the matter is time-critical.

**Pitfalls** — Treating it as a firm yes; pushing "so is that a yes or no?" in public, which costs both of you face.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Can you get me the permit by Monday?" / "I will try, ya…" / "Appreciate it. If Monday looks tight, just let me know early." The last line opens the honest exit.

### Salam, the two-handed handshake

**Context** — First meetings, Raya visits, formal receptions. The traditional Malay greeting: offer both hands to wrap the other person's right hand, slight bow, then draw your own right hand to your chest. Same-gender friends may also grip with both hands.

**Reading** — A full salam signals warmth and respect; hand-to-heart afterwards says "I greet you sincerely". An elder may offer the back of his hand for you to touch lightly to your forehead or nose (salam cium tangan).

**Response options**
1. Mirror the gesture: wrap, bow slightly, hand to heart.
2. If unsure of the elder's intention, watch for the offered hand-back and respond to that.
3. Verbal layer: "Assalamualaikum" with Muslims; "Selamat sejahtera" works for anyone.

**Pitfalls** — A bone-crushing Western handshake; grabbing a woman's hand uninvited; skipping the greeting and going straight to business.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: At a Raya open house you take pakcik's offered hand, touch it lightly to your forehead: "Selamat Hari Raya, pakcik." He beams and pulls you toward the food.

### When she doesn't extend her hand

**Context** — You meet a Malay woman; you offer a handshake; she smiles and nods without taking it.

**Reading** — Not coldness. Many Malay Muslim women avoid physical contact with men; the nod and smile already complete the greeting.

**Response options**
1. Withdraw smoothly, right hand to your chest, slight nod, continue the introduction.
2. Use her title in the first sentence: "Puan", "Cik", or "Ustazah" as appropriate.
3. Keep a polite distance for documents and card exchanges; use your right hand or both.

**Pitfalls** — Re-offering the hand or joking "a handshake won't hurt"; any touch, shoulder pats included.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: You extend a hand to Puan Aminah; she presses both palms together and nods. You draw back, hand to chest: "Puan Aminah, selamat sejahtera. Good to meet you." Business proceeds normally.

### Getting the titles right

**Context** — Names arrive wrapped in honorifics: Tun, Tan Sri, Dato', Datuk, Datin, Encik, Puan, Cik, Ustaz, plus styles like Yang Berhormat for ministers.

**Reading** — Titles are earned or hereditary and their misuse is a visible slight. Tun outranks Tan Sri, which outranks Datuk/Dato'; a woman titled in her own right is Datin Paduka; spouses carry their own forms (Toh Puan for Tun's wife, Puan Sri for Tan Sri's wife, Datin for Datuk/Dato').

**Response options**
1. When in doubt, go formal: "Dato' Ahmad", "Puan Lim" — people rarely resent too much respect.
2. Mirror whatever title the person uses when introducing himself.
3. For written invitations and emails, keep the full title in the salutation.

**Pitfalls** — Shortening "Dato'" to plain "Ahmad" or mixing up Datin and Datin Paduka; asking "so which one is higher, Datuk or Dato'?" in front of the titleholder.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: Agenda lists "YBhg. Dato' Sri Rahim". In the meeting you say "Dato' Sri" every time you address him, and everything runs smoother than your last visit.

### Left-hand rules

**Context** — Passing food, handing over documents or money, pointing at anything, receiving a gift.

**Reading** — The left hand is reserved for washing after the toilet, so giving, receiving, pointing, or eating with it alone reads as unclean or disrespectful. Both hands together is the safest and most respectful form.

**Response options**
1. Default to two hands for anything you pass or receive.
2. If one hand only, make it the right.
3. When pointing, use the full right hand, palm open, or the right thumb with fingers folded.

**Pitfalls** — Handing your business card or a shoe with the left hand; pointing at a person with the index finger.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: At lunch you pass the sambal with your right hand, fingers supporting the underside of the dish. Nobody comments, which is the point.

### Shoes at the door

**Context** — You arrive at a Malay home, or any house with shoes scattered outside; also mosques and many small shops and offices.

**Reading** — Footwear is unclean because floors double as prayer space; leaving shoes outside is basic respect, not optional.

**Response options**
1. Look down: if shoes are parked outside, yours join them.
2. Check your socks before you go; a hole is your problem for the whole visit.
3. Carry a small bag rather than a rolling suitcase into a home visit.

**Pitfalls** — Stepping in wearing shoes "just for a second"; placing your shoes on the wrong side blocking the door.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: Ten pairs of sandals line the porch of the open house. You add yours, straighten them, and step in barefoot with your gift held in both hands.

### The buah tangan visit

**Context** — You're invited to a Malay home for a meal, Raya, or a kenduri. Guests often arrive slightly late and unannounced visits are normal.

**Reading** — Hospitality is the host's honor. Bring a small gift (buah tangan, "fruit of the hands"): halal pastries, fruit, chocolates. Wait for the host to invite you to eat. Leave a morsel on your plate to signal you are full; a polished plate earns you a refill.

**Response options**
1. Hand the gift with both hands and let the host set it aside unopened.
2. Praise the food specifically: "The rendang is sedap" beats a generic thanks.
3. If you must decline food, do it with your hand on your stomach and a smile: "Sudah kenyang, terima kasih."

**Pitfalls** — Arriving with alcohol, a pork product, or a dog-themed souvenir; refusing every dish; eating while walking around the house.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: You bring a box of dates and kuih. Host: "Ala, you didn't have to!" You: "Small buah tangan only." It's placed on the sideboard, unopened, and lunch begins.

### Ramadan and the calendar of sensitivities

**Context** — Scheduling meetings, meals, and site visits through the Muslim calendar: Ramadan fasting hours, Raya holidays, Friday prayers.

**Reading** — During Ramadan, colleagues fast from dawn to dusk; daytime lunches are off and energy dips before buka puasa (the fast-breaking meal). Raya week empties offices.

**Response options**
1. Move working lunches to after 6:30pm during Ramadan, or book rooms away from food.
2. Don't hand snacks or insist a fasting colleague "just have a bite".
3. When an event lands on Raya, reschedule or send a wish: "Selamat Hari Raya, maaf zahir dan batin."

**Pitfalls** — Scheduling a client feast on the first day of Raya; teasing a colleague about breakfast.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: Ramadan team dinner moved to 7pm; your Muslim teammates eat at buka puasa and everyone toasts with sirap. The calendar note in your invite saved the evening.

### Hierarchy in the room

**Context** — Meetings with elders, officials, or titled individuals; the oldest or most senior person expects to be greeted first and addressed by title.

**Reading** — Malays place strangers in a hierarchy quickly (who are you attached to? how old? what religion?) and calibrate language accordingly. Deference to elders is structural, not decorative: don't cross your legs in front of them, don't smoke near them, lower your head slightly when passing.

**Response options**
1. Greet the most senior person first, by title, before working the room.
2. Let the senior counterpart open the meeting; volunteer your view when invited.
3. Disagree privately afterwards, one-on-one, never across the table.

**Pitfalls** — Correcting an elder in public; sitting with legs crossed or the soles of your feet visible toward your host.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: You enter the office, greet Dato' first with a salam, then the team. Mid-meeting the Dato' misses a figure; you note it in the minutes instead of raising it aloud, and follow up with him at tea.
