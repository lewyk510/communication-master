### The polite "yes" that isn't a commitment

**Context** — Global English (ELF) calls with suppliers, partners, or colleagues across time zones; medium stakes.

**Reading** — Three candidates: (1) an acknowledgment token meaning "I heard you"; (2) a face-saving fill to keep the call moving; (3) a genuine commitment. Only (3) justifies waiting silently.

**Response options**
1. Convert speech into a checkable artifact: "Can you post that in the channel or email me with a date?"
2. Ask for a partial deliverable: "What part of it can you have by Wednesday?"
3. If it lapses twice, hold a no-blame review: "Do we define 'done' the same way?"

**Pitfalls** — Reading "yes" through your own culture's promise-weight; ELF calls flatten prosody, so tone carries less signal than you assume.

**Examples** — `constructed example` A PM waited three weeks on a verbal "yes"; after switching to Friday written confirmations, slippage dropped from two weeks to two days.

### The silence that isn't rudeness

**Context** — A low-context counterpart goes quiet on chat or email overnight; low-to-medium stakes.

**Reading** — (1) Different work-hours boundary, low-context cultures often hard-stop at end of day; (2) they are waiting for a document, not a chat; (3) genuine deprioritization. Options (1) and (2) dominate.

**Response options**
1. Send one low-context follow-up: "Confirming receipt; do you need anything else from me?"
2. Replace any "please confirm verbally" with a checkable in-message ask.
3. After two working days, switch channel once rather than assigning negative intent.

**Pitfalls** — Inferring hostility from timezone asymmetry; "they ignored me" is usually arithmetic, not emotion.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "Did he get upset?"... "It's 2 a.m. in Munich." Case closed.

### The direct feedback that feels like an attack

**Context** — A supervisor from a low-context, low-power-distance culture (Dutch, German, Israeli, parts of US) criticizes your work in front of the group.

**Reading** — Primary: it is task-critique, which they treat as respecting your intellect; secondary: targeted dislike. Test: do they do this to everyone?

**Response options**
1. Treat it as an invitation: answer with reasons or data on the spot, no heat.
2. Verify afterwards: "Were those comments direction-setting or just for discussion?"
3. If you need face, negotiate a rule: "I'd rather go through these one-to-one after the meeting." Most such managers accept it.

**Pitfalls** — Coding public correction as humiliation, then going cold; the other side never learns why the relationship iced over.

**Examples** — `constructed example` A Singapore team's new Dutch head dissects a plan publicly each quarter; the misread lasted two weeks until one one-to-one.

### The CC to your boss

**Context** — A Western colleague loops your manager in on a thread about your work.

**Reading** — In low-context, individualist settings this is usually accountability, not aggression: keep the boss informed, document, no offense intended.

**Response options**
1. Match transparency rather than escalate: "Posting progress here so everyone's aligned."
2. Privately propose: "Let's keep this between us and escalate together if needed."
3. If they CC everyone habitually, send your own proactive summaries to remove the trigger.

**Pitfalls** — Retaliating via back-channels; or being afraid to loop in your own manager and losing the information advantage.

**Examples** — `constructed example` One side read a CC as an attack, responded with private messages only, was read as "opaque", and both camped into a spiral.

### "Let me check and get back to you"

**Context** — Verbal face-to-face or call with an indirect-style colleague; medium stakes, deadline involved.

**Reading** — Three candidates: a genuine check; a polite no (ontology: the answer will never arrive); a request for time to consult others, which indirect cultures expect before saying yes.

**Response options**
1. Add a checkpoint rather than press: "Shall I check back with you on Friday?"
2. Offer an out: "If it's not going to work, no worries, just let me know." This often converts a polite no into an honest one.
3. If stakes are high, ask about the decision process: "Who else needs to be consulted before this can land?"

**Pitfalls** — Treating the phrase as both a yes and a no depending on your mood that day; the phrase only has stable meaning inside the other person's system.

**Examples** — `constructed example` Weeks of chasing, resolved when the asker offered a no-cost exit and received "it's not going to work this quarter" within a day.

### "Insya Allah / will try" as an answer

**Context** — Malaysia-based collaboration, common reply pattern; cross-references [[cultures/cu1-malaysia-malay/core]].

**Reading** — (1) Willingness with soft commitment, religious idiom plus hedging; (2) a plain yes; (3) a polite improbability. Misreading this as "unreliable" is the top expat complaint about Malaysian counterparts, and the top local complaint about expats' bluntness.

**Response options**
1. Light follow-up without interrogation: "Great, let's glance at progress on Thursday."
2. If stakes are high, swap mechanism, not tone: "Could you share where it stands right now?"
3. Calibrate on the person's own track record, never on a barang label.

**Pitfalls** — Treating a religious stock phrase as an excuse; using mocking tones around it in a Malaysian workplace is a real escalation risk.

**Examples** — `constructed example` A foreign vendor pressed "so yes or no?" in every meeting; the local counterpart stopped volunteering details in return.

### translated politeness that lands wrong

**Context** — You wrote a request and translated it from your first language; medium stakes, cross-cultural recipient.

**Reading** — Direct translations of honorific style (e.g., over-literal humble forms) can land as sarcasm, servility, or confusion; the functional politeness did not survive.

**Response options**
1. Translate the function, not the form: raising the other, softening imposition, leaving a graceful exit.
2. Use the target language's native hedges that carry the same burden ("would you mind", "if it's not too much trouble").
3. When unsure, replace honorific height with specificity: a precise, small request reads polite almost everywhere.

**Pitfalls** — Importing your language's deference hierarchy into a language where the equivalent phrase is mocking; over-marking politeness in ELF reads as condescension.

**Examples** — `constructed example` A literal translation of a Chinese deferential opener into English made a US contact reply "seems sarcastic?" and the collaboration cooled before clarification.

### The "we really want to see what…" in ELF negotiation

**Context** — Multilingual business English where non-native speakers negotiate price or scope.

**Reading** — In ELF, unusual phrasing usually signals limited lexicon, not hidden intent; but some phrases do carry real hedging ("we will see", "next time"). Separating lexis limits from strategy requires context.

**Response options**
1. Restate in plain forms and confirm: "Just to be sure: does that mean a yes, a maybe, or a no?"
2. Time-box optionally: "Let's hold this open until Friday, and either way we close it then."
3. Watch for repeated confirmation requests from the other side; in ELF they want meaning nailed down, not small talk.

**Pitfalls** — Mistaking a non-native's flat tone for coldness; ELF speakers often drop intonation and politeness particles not because they are rude but because they are saving processing power.

**Examples** — `constructed example` A native speaker read a Finnish counterpart's bluntness as hostility; the Finnish side read the native speaker's hedging as evasiveness. Both were using their own defaults.

### Ambiguous cross-cultural signal: ask, don't guess

**Context** — You must interpret an ambiguous behavior whose cultural frame you do not know.

**Reading** — No default reading; the reading lives in the five axes: relationship, power, stakes, culture, goal (see core.md question bank).

**Response options**
1. Ask the user 3–5 targeted questions first, per core.md's S2 bank.
2. If answers are limited, output 2–3 candidate readings with confidence levels, and say plainly what you cannot infer.
3. Recommend the user run a cheap direct check with the person themselves.

**Pitfalls** — National averages are distributions, not verdicts; the sentence pattern "people from X always…" is out of bounds. Frameworks supply candidates; only the person themselves supplies evidence.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "Indian client said wonderful but hasn't ordered in three weeks?" First question: "Did you send a written quote and payment terms?", not a bargaining stereotype.
