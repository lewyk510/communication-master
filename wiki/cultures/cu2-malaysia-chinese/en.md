### "Aunty/Uncle" — the age-tier address

**Context** — Addressing Chinese strangers and acquaintances of your parents' generation: hawker centres, wet markets, friends' homes.

**Reading** — It is a respect tier, not a family claim. Used correctly it warms the interaction instantly. Used on someone too young it lands as an insult.

**Response options**
1. Guess senior: "Uncle, how much for this?"
2. Guess young and upgrade if corrected (safer direction).
3. Use titles first for professionals: Dr., lawyer, Dato'.

**Pitfalls** — Calling a 40-something woman "aunty"; dropping the address entirely and going straight to "you there" reads rude.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Aunty, the char kway teow less spicy can? ... Wah, so fast!" The vendor now treats you as a regular.

### Ang pow math

**Context** — Chinese New Year, weddings, home visits. You hand cash in a red packet.

**Reading** — Even numbers bless; odd numbers evoke funerals; 4 (sounds like "death") is out; 8, 88, 168 are lucky (Evidence-backed, FMT). Wedding packets track the banquet's per-head price; underpaying is publicly visible.

**Response options**
1. Ask a Chinese friend the going rate for that venue.
2. Kids at CNY: RM2–50 by closeness, fresh notes only.
3. Receiving: both hands, bless the giver back ("Gong Xi Fa Cai").

**Pitfalls** — Wrinkled or old notes; coins; opening the packet in front of the giver; a green duit raya packet at a Chinese event.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: RM88 for a colleague's kid beats RM100? No: 100 is even and fine, but 88 flirts with luck; 84 does not exist.

### "Which school did you go to?"

**Context** — Standard icebreaker among Malaysian Chinese; also asked of your kids.

**Reading** — They are asking for the school's *name and type*: SJKC (Chinese primary), SMJK, Chinese independent school (独中), or national school. The answer indexes upbringing, networks, and Chinese literacy (Evidence-backed, Wikipedia: ~90% Chinese children attend Mandarin-medium primary schools).

**Response options**
1. Answer with name + type: "SJKC Chung Hua, then a Chinese independent school in JB."
2. Compliment upward: "SJKC kids' maths is no joke."
3. If national-schooled, pivot to languages: "National school, but I picked up Mandarin from my neighbours."

**Pitfalls** — Treating the question as classist interrogation; mocking Mandarin-accented English (a default accent, not a defect).

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "You went to Chong Hwa? Independent? Then UEC la." "Yes." "Respect, that syllabus is brutal."

### Fighting over the bill

**Context** — Group meals end and two or three people physically block each other from paying.

**Reading** — Paying is a face move and a renqing deposit; losing the fight is fine because the *winner* now owes the table a future meal. You do not actually split evenly among close friends.

**Response options**
1. Lose gracefully and deposit: "OK you win, next one is on me."
2. Win then immediately name the sequel: "This one me. Next round, dim sum, your treat."
3. For recurring groups: rotate loudly, "Uncle Lee paid last time, it's me."

**Pitfalls** — Calculating your share to the sen among friends (fine in the office, stingy at the family table); letting an elder pay *your* celebratory meal without protest.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: Both hands push the terminal away. "I pay, I pay!" "No, no..." Card is already tapped. "You really ah... OK next time you cannot fight me."

### Soft refusals: "we'll see", "next time la"

**Context** — You invite, offer, or ask for a favour; the reply is vague.

**Reading** — Three candidates: (1) polite no, (2) genuine maybe, (3) yes but they need to save face around a constraint. Face culture makes direct "no" expensive for both sides.

**Response options**
1. Accept the ambiguity, follow up once later by text: "No pressure, but if Saturday works I'll book."
2. Offer a face-saving exit: "If this week is packed, next month totally fine."
3. Watch for the second soft no — after two, it is a no. Stop asking.

**Pitfalls** — Forcing an answer on the spot ("just tell me yes or no") converts a soft no into a relationship bruise; taking "next time la" as a confirmed RSVP.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Come to Penang stay at my place!" "Next time la." Book a hotel. The invitation was real warmth, not a booking.

### Give face: correct in private, praise in public

**Context** — Meetings, family gatherings, group chats. Someone (elder, boss, host) makes a mistake in front of others.

**Reading** — Public correction strips face; face is conferred by others' regard, so the audience matters more than the facts (Evidence-backed, sociologist Zhai Xuewei via Beijing News interview). A muffled error everyone pretends not to see is the norm, not cowardice.

**Response options**
1. Absorb the error now, message privately after.
2. Re-route praise to cover it: "Actually what Encik said earlier about timing is the key point..."
3. If it must be flagged now, question your own understanding instead: "Help me check, I read it as X?"

**Pitfalls** — "Actually, that's wrong" in the group chat; over-crediting yourself when you save someone's face.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: Boss misquotes the figure. You stay silent, then WhatsApp: "Boss, the number was 88, just so your deck is clean for tomorrow."

### The renqing ledger in business

**Context** — Deals, suppliers, clients, festivals. Gifts and favours move before contracts do.

**Reading** — Reciprocity (礼尚往来) is the operating system: receive a gift, return one of matching weight later; never repaying signals you are out of the relationship (Evidence-backed, Cheng & Tsui). Lavish gifts embarrass the receiver (unreturnable) and can trip anti-bribery lines at their workplace.

**Response options**
1. Match weight, not exceed: cookies for cookies, mooncakes for mooncakes.
2. Bank small favours ("I know a guy") and let them mature.
3. When refused a gift once, offer again; refused twice, withdraw with grace.

**Pitfalls** — Invoicing a favour instantly; one-way extraction from a "guanxi" you never feed; expensive gifts to civil servants.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: Client sends a hamper at CNY. You note the brand tier, and send the same tier at Mid-Autumn. The next deal's coffee meetings get friendlier.

### Dialect badges and the "are you Hokkien" opener

**Context** — Meeting another Malaysian Chinese person; small talk before business.

**Reading** — "What's your clan / where are you from?" asks for the dialect group: Hokkien, Cantonese, Hakka, Teochew, Hainanese (Evidence-backed, Wikipedia census shares: Hokkien ~32%, Hakka ~22%, Cantonese ~20%). It predicts their home language and often their hometown's food.

**Response options**
1. Answer clan + place: "Hokkien, from Klang."
2. Guess playfully: "Teochew, right? You love your teochew porridge."
3. Bridge to food: "Klang Hokkien? Then you know real bak kut teh."

**Pitfalls** — Answering "Malaysian" (they mean clan); calling dialects "broken Mandarin"; assuming Penang and JB Hokkien are the same (north has Malay loans, south keeps more China forms — Evidence-backed, Wikipedia).

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "You Hakka?" "Yes, Sabah one." "Ah, KK! No wonder your family cooks so much ginger."
