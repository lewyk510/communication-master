English-lane signals for the live service counter, phone and chat: half customer-side (what the agent's line really means, and how to answer), half frontline-side (handling angry customers). Mechanisms and the Malaysian escalation ladder live in [[scenarios/sc9-live-service/core]].

### "That's not my department"

**Context** — First-round deflection at a counter or on a call; common in banks, telcos, mall service desks. Tone stays polite while the ball leaves their court.

**Reading** — (1) Genuine split of duties, and they hold the transfer right; (2) deflection: they could handle it but won't; (3) a stalling tactic betting you'll give up.

**Response options**
1. Pin down ownership: "Then please connect me to the team that does, and give me a reference number for today."
2. Leave a mark: "Please note on record that I raised this with you today and was told it's not your area."
3. Put it in writing: "Kindly send me that in writing so I can attach it if I file with TTPM."

**Pitfalls** — Hanging up and redialing restarts the case from zero. Accepting a transfer with no reference number means the next agent can't see a thing.

**Examples** — constructed example: Customer reports a wrongful card charge; teller says "not our department." Customer replies with option 2; teller softens into "let me at least log it for you."

### "It's company policy"

**Context** — The standard shield when a return is refused, a fee is contested, or a refund timeline stretches. Classic from agents reading off a script.

**Reading** — (1) The agent genuinely has no discretion; (2) discretion exists but they fear responsibility; (3) the policy is real and you missed a step (receipt, deadline, condition).

**Response options**
1. Ask for the exception-holder: "Policy covers the normal case. My situation is (one-line fact). Who has the authority to approve an exception?"
2. Demand the text: "Could you show me that policy in writing, or send it over?" Verbal policy and written terms often disagree; only the written one counts.
3. Accept and lock a deadline: "Fine, follow the process then. By that process, what do I receive and by what date?"

**Pitfalls** — Arguing with the policy loses; it can't bend. The movable object is always "whose authority can change the outcome."

**Examples** — constructed example: Staff says "opened items are non-refundable." Customer asks for the written clause, which actually reads "excluding defective goods." Photos of the defect get shown; the exception is approved.

### "I've noted it down" (no reference number)

**Context** — The universal phone-agent buffer, delivered exactly when you expect a commitment.

**Reading** — (1) A ticket genuinely exists but they forgot to give you the number; (2) a soothing phrase with nothing behind it; (3) logged, but parked where it will never escalate.

**Response options**
1. Ask for the number: "May I have the case reference number?" The single highest-value sentence in this scenario.
2. Interrogate the claim: "Logged where, handled by whom, within how many days?"
3. Convert to writing: "Please send the summary of what you just logged to my phone so I can check it."

**Pitfalls** — Hearing "noted" as "solved." Logging is storage; stored cases age until someone chases them.

**Examples** — constructed example: "All right, I've made a note of that." "What's the reference number?" "Er... let me check." That one-second pause is the difference between a script and a ticket.

### "There's nothing I can do"

**Context** — The frontline helplessness line, usually at the exact moment a workaround exists. Counter, phone and chat alike.

**Reading** — (1) True: their screen shows no options; (2) false: options exist but require effort or risk; (3) a polite wish for you to accept the loss.

**Response options**
1. Split person from position: "I'm not asking you to break rules. Who in this branch can approve it, and how do I reach them?"
2. Offer the easier path: "If you can't, I'll escalate in writing and this call becomes the first entry in the record."
3. Ask for the ceiling: "What is the most you are authorised to do right now?" Often unlocks a partial remedy instantly.

**Pitfalls** — Treating the sentence as final. "Nothing I can do" is a statement about one screen, not about the organisation.

**Examples** — constructed example: Agent repeats the line three times. Customer: "What CAN you do?" Agent pauses, then offers a partial credit they'd been authorised to give all along.

### The apology with no remedy

**Context** — "We're sorry for the inconvenience" delivered as a conclusion rather than an opening. Very common in chat support.

**Reading** — (1) Scripted empathy filler; (2) they have no authority to compensate, so apology is all they hold; (3) they hope the apology closes the case.

**Response options**
1. Accept and redirect: "Thank you. Now that we've covered the apology, what's the fix? I need (amount/refund/repair) by (date)."
2. Name the gap: "An apology is free and my loss isn't. What does sorry convert into?"
3. Give them the script to escalate: "If sorry is all your level can offer, pass the case up with my notes attached."

**Pitfalls** — Accepting the apology as closure. Service-recovery research shows satisfaction tracks the outcome and the process speed (distributive and procedural justice), not the warmth of the wording (Evidence-backed: Lim, Saha & Das 2025).

**Examples** — constructed example: "We sincerely apologise for the delay." "Thank you. The remedy I'm asking for is a full refund of RM189 within 7 days. Can you process that or pass it to someone who can?"

### The dangling "we'll refund in 7-14 working days"

**Context** — Refund promises with a range and no anchor: no date, no confirmation channel, no failure remedy.

**Reading** — (1) Honest queue time with backend variance; (2) a range chosen to blur the real deadline; (3) an exit line they expect you not to verify.

**Response options**
1. Collapse the range: "Which date applies to me? Please confirm in writing: amount, date, and original payment channel."
2. Attach a failure clause: "If it hasn't arrived by that date, I'll treat the matter as unresolved and escalate with this chat as evidence."
3. Get the transaction ID: "Give me the refund transaction reference so I can trace it with my bank."

**Pitfalls** — Ranges convert to deadlines only when you pick the near end out loud. Silent acceptance usually lands on day 14.

**Examples** — constructed example: "7 to 14 working days." "Please confirm day 7 in writing." The agent books "7 working days, confirmed by email" — the anchor moved to the near end because the customer asked.

### Angry customer at your counter (frontline)

**Context** — Your side of the desk. Minute one: volume high, facts tangled, everyone in the queue watching.

**Reading** — (1) Anger at the situation; you are simply the first human to absorb it; (2) anger at the company: a prior failure, this is round two; (3) rarely, anger at you personally, which usually reveals itself only after the facts come out.

**Response options**
1. Let the first 60 seconds finish, then label: "I can hear how frustrating this has been. Go ahead, I'm listening, and I'll sort it out."
2. Reflect it back as facts: "Let me confirm: order X, problem Y, you need Z by tomorrow. Correct?" Volume converts into information.
3. Promise a concrete first action: "Give me two minutes and I'll come back with something specific."

**Pitfalls** — "Please calm down" reads as a verdict on their manners and buys you a second, louder outburst. Apologising without a remedy sets up disappointment number two; satisfaction runs on what they get and how fast (Evidence-backed: Lim, Saha & Das 2025).

**Examples** — constructed example: Customer storms up shouting about terrible service. Agent listens, then: "Third trip for the same thing, I'd be furious too. Give me your order number, two minutes." Volume drops immediately.

### Customer threatens to post about it / go to TTPM (frontline)

**Context** — Escalation phase. The customer mentions social media exposure, a consumer group, or the Tribunal.

**Reading** — (1) They know the process and hold evidence: treat as high risk; (2) venting: they want to matter, not to litigate; (3) probing your authority ceiling for an above-policy remedy.

**Response options**
1. Translate the threat into a need: "So what you want is (refund/exchange/apology), right? That's within my authority, I can do it now."
2. Show respect, show speed: "TTPM is absolutely your right, and you don't need a lawyer for it. Meanwhile I can fix this here in ten minutes. Which is faster for you?"
3. Hand off cleanly when over your ceiling: "This amount is above my limit. I'm connecting you to the duty manager now; here's my staff ID."

**Pitfalls** — Snapping "go ahead, sue" is self-harm: the Tribunal covers claims up to RM50,000, the filing fee is RM5, and no lawyer is required (Evidence-backed: TTPM official pages). Fighting a winnable-in-ten-minutes case costs the company far more. Also never buy peace with commitments beyond your authority; that debt comes back to you personally.

**Examples** — constructed example: "Fix this or I'm going on Facebook." Agent: "Posting is your right. I'd rather close it today: new unit today, or full refund in three days. Your call." Customer takes the exchange.

### The bot loop where no human appears

**Context** — Chat widget or hotline IVR where every path ends in a scripted answer. The complaint never reaches a person who can decide.

**Reading** — (1) Genuine tiering: the system wants cheap channels used first; (2) deliberate friction: attrition by chatbot; (3) outage: the human queue is genuinely broken.

**Response options**
1. Force the human keyword: type "agent", "human", "complaint", "TTPM" — complaint language routes to humans faster than problem language.
2. Jump channels: email and the regulator's portal route outside the bot loop entirely (e.g. CFM's aduan.mcmc.gov.my for telecom, aduan@cfm.my, 1800-188-030; Evidence-backed, CFM official).
3. Write the paper trail instead: "Since no human is available, my next step is a formal written complaint with regulator cc."

**Pitfalls** — Spending an hour rephrasing the question for the bot. The bot's job is to resolve; if it hasn't in fifteen minutes, the correct move is sideways or upward, not deeper into the same loop.

**Examples** — constructed example: Twenty minutes of bot answers about a dead fibre line. Customer emails a complaint citing CFM's escalation path and cc's screenshots; a human calls back the same afternoon.
