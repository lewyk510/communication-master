# Workplace Power · English Signal Handbook

Anglo workplace register: how power is signaled and negotiated with bosses, peers, and reports in English-speaking offices. Written for English speakers directly, not translated from the zh or ms lanes.

### "Do you have a quick minute?"

**Context** — The classic ambush opener from a boss or senior colleague. "Quick" and "minute" are doing a lot of load-bearing work; the actual ask is usually neither.

**Reading**
1. Genuinely brief: one question, one answer.
2. Opens a bigger issue that needs a real block of time.
3. Escalation in disguise: something above you has landed on your desk.

**Response options**
1. Bound the minute while accepting: "Sure. I've got about 15 before my 3 o'clock. What's up?"
2. If this is the third "quick minute" this week: "Can we do 30 minutes Thursday? Last few minutes kept spilling over."
3. Say yes but hold your line: "Give me five to finish this email."

**Pitfalls** — Saying "sure!" to every quick minute quietly hands your calendar to other people. The real cost is the context-switch, not the minutes.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Do you have a quick minute?" → "I'm free right after the 2pm stand-up. Want me to drop by then?" preserves your schedule without refusing the boss.

### "Not a bad idea" / "Interesting"

**Context** — You present a plan to a senior person in a meeting or over email. You get two words back and a silence.

**Reading**
1. Genuine lukewarm approval, nothing more to add.
2. Polite rejection reserved for later ("interesting" minus follow-up questions is the tell).
3. They're still thinking and don't want to commit on the spot.

**Response options**
1. Convert the hedge into criteria: "Good. What would need to be true for you to fully back it?"
2. Offer a smaller next step: "Want me to scope just phase one for next week?"
3. Let it sit; check their behavior on it in a week rather than the words.

**Pitfalls** — Walking away thinking you got a green light because they didn't say no. Silence plus a hedge is usually a delayed no.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Interesting." → You follow with "What are your main reservations?" and the reservations arrive within seconds. The word was a buffer, not a verdict.

### "Per my last email"

**Context** — The most studied passive-aggressive construction in office email. It means "you didn't read what I wrote," without ever saying it.

**Reading**
1. Genuine frustration vented through formality.
2. A paper-trail move before an escalation: they are documenting that they already told you.
3. Habitual phrasing from someone who writes to a template.

**Response options**
1. Answer the content, ignore the jab: "Ah, I missed it in the thread. Confirming: yes, X ships Friday."
2. Name it lightly once if it's a pattern with a peer: "I know threads get long. Going forward I'll bold the ask."
3. If it's your manager, just answer cleanly and let it pass; correcting tone upward is rarely worth it.

**Pitfalls** — Responding with "per your last email" back. The regression chain goes forever and nobody wins.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Per my last email, the vendor needs a decision today." → "Confirmed, approving X today. (Sorry for the missed earlier reply, got buried.)" Defuses the jab without conceding on substance.

### The boss says "we'll look into it"

**Context** — You raised a real problem (understaffing, a toxic process, a pay inequity) and got a vague assurance. Common outcome in any hierarchy; worse in high power-distance ones.

**Reading**
1. Genuine intent, low priority: it will die in their backlog.
2. A soft no to protect you from hard no.
3. They are taking it higher and can't say that yet.

**Response options**
1. Time-box it yourself: "Understood. Can I check back with you in three weeks if I haven't heard anything?"
2. Convert to a named artifact: "Should I put this in writing in a one-pager so the context isn't lost?"
3. Accept, privately set your own deadline for re-escalation or routing around.

**Pitfalls** — Treating the verbal assurance as an action item in your own plan. Assurances aren't commitments; written dates are.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "We'll look into resourcing." → You send the one-pager Friday, then calendar "re-ask" for three weeks out. If it dies twice, you have your answer.

### A peer takes credit in the meeting

**Context** — You did the analysis; in front of the VP, a peer says "my recommendation" or presents your numbers without naming you. One of the highest-frequency office politics events.

**Reading**
1. Habitual "I/we" compression; they pile credit by accident forever.
2. Deliberate brand-building at your expense.
3. They legitimately own the project and you own a work package; scope is blurrier than it feels.

**Response options**
1. Preempt: keep deliverables on timestamped written channels (email CC, weekly update) so the record exists before anyone speaks.
2. Same meeting, one non-hostile sentence: "Adding to that, the data I pulled shows..." — visible without accusing.
3. Badger in private later: "Next time that deck goes to the VP, could you name the folks behind the analysis?"

**Pitfalls** — Confronting publicly. In front of the VP, observers remember the disruptor, not the credit-taker. Silence forever is worse though: it converts one incident into a precedent.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: Weekly note: "Completed X attrition analysis (with P)." Four months later in the review cycle, that one line is your evidence chain.

### The 9pm WhatsApp ping

**Context** — A manager messages after hours, "just checking" or "no rush." The channel choice, not the words, is the message: it colonizes your evening.

**Reading**
1. Actually urgent: outages, clients, things that genuinely can't wait.
2. Scope creep as culture: they send whenever they think of it and expect replies whenever.
3. Testing your responsiveness as a stamina probe.

**Response options**
1. Answer urgent-looking ones instantly, and non-urgent ones at 9am next day with a visible timestamp. The delay itself teaches the pattern.
2. Set the boundary once, warmly: "Quick note on hours. I check WhatsApp from 9 to 9. If something's on fire, call me."
3. Move work chatter to official channels where your team's working-hours norms get enforced automatically.

**Pitfalls** — Replying at 11pm "just this once." Once per week becomes the expectation. Silence for three days when things crash is the opposite trap that proves you were being tested.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: Friday 8:47pm: "Any thoughts on the deck?" → Saturday 10:05am: "Had thoughts! Two structural notes + one suggestion. Can walk you through at 10:30."

### Giving feedback upward (managing up the feedback channel)

**Context** — Your boss does something that costs the team (late inputs, changed priorities mid-week, hijacking meetings). In high power-distance settings, direct vertical feedback is risky; skip it and you internalize the cost. CCL's research lines up here: feedback conversations that name situation, behavior, and impact reduce defensiveness on both sides. Evidence-backed, see [[scenarios/sc1-workplace-power/core]].

**Reading**
1. Boss is unaware: the behavior is a habit, not a choice.
2. Boss is aware and has constraints you can't see.
3. Boss knows and doesn't care (rare; usually a performance conversation goes sideways toward option 3 fast).

**Response options**
1. SBI structure, wrapped in inquiry: "In yesterday's client call (S), when the numbers changed mid-presentation (B), the team spent the afternoon re-verifying rather than closing next steps (I). What's the best way to lock inputs earlier?"
2. Do it in private, never in meetings; upward feedback in public is a threat display.
3. Ask for permission to raise it: "Can I give you some feedback on the review cycle?" The yes buys you less defensiveness.

**Pitfalls** — Opening with "you always..." The universal quantifier converts feedback into an attack. Never give your boss an adjective to defend against.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "You talk over the juniors in reviews" → "In Monday's review (S), when you jumped in after the first 30 seconds (B), Dana shut down for the rest of it (I). Could we try a two-minute silence rule?"

### Disagreeing with the boss without detonating

**Context** — Mid-meeting, your boss proposes a direction you think is wrong. In high power-distance workplaces, open contradiction sits closer to insubordination than to participation; but silence is not neutral either — systems that punish speaking up pay for it in errors and accidents. Evidence-backed on cost of silence, see [[scenarios/sc1-workplace-power/core]].

**Reading**
1. Boss wants red-team input and is testing whether you provide it.
2. Boss has already decided and is informing, not consulting.
3. Boss is wrong for reasons you haven't fully surfaced yet.

**Response options**
1. Shared-goal pivot: "We both want this shipped in Q3. The client data points the other way on [X] — can I lay out what I'm seeing?" Then hand the decision back: "If we stay the course, I'll execute fully."
2. Ask a question instead of making a claim: "What have we seen from the last two pilots that supports this?"
3. Use time as an ally: park it as a written follow-up instead of a live debate.

**Pitfalls** — Winning the argument publicly and losing the year: even a maximal-gracious boss will register the face-loss. Also fatal: hedging so hard your view becomes unrecognizable.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "That won't work" → "We both want this in Q3. I'm seeing two client signals pointing the other way. Want me to write them up before we commit?"

### The raise/promotion conversation

**Context** — Asking for a raise or promotion. Mixed evidence situations: budget cycles, visible wins, market offers.

**Reading** (before the ask, read the room)
1. There is a cycle or a pool and you're in it, they were waiting for the ask.
2. No pool and no budget: the answer is constrained regardless of your worth.
3. They know you have an external offer and are reacting to that, not to you.

**Response options**
1. Bring evidence, not emotion: "Last year I shipped X, Y, Z; peers at this level in market sit at A–B range; what's the gap to next level for me?"
2. Ask for the roadmap rather than the number: "What specifically would make me unambiguous for the next level?" A no with a map beats a yes with nothing.
3. Let external offers do the negotiating they are designed for, but never bluff one you don't hold.

**Pitfalls** — Building the case on tenure ("I've been here 5 years") instead of delivery. Threatening to leave without an offer in hand. Picking the boss's worst week (quarter close, post-mortem).

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "I think I'm underpaid" → "Across the two client deliverables and the on-call work this year, here's the impact list. What's the path and timeline to the next band?"

### Resigning: the written letter beats the dramatic exit

**Context** — You've decided to leave. The mechanics matter more than the feelings. SHRM's operational checklist: have the end date documented in the resignation letter, give two weeks' notice, and "be careful how you give notice." Resignation-in-icing-on-a-cake and other viral exits keep careers short and references cold. Evidence-backed, see [[scenarios/sc1-workplace-power/core]].

**Reading**
1. Employer may terminate immediately upon notice; know your contract's notice terms both ways.
2. The two weeks are for the record as much as the transition: leave as the professional they'd want as a reference.
3. Anything posted publicly is permanent: "once it goes out into the Internet, it's in indelible ink."

**Response options**
1. Formal written letter first (statement, final day, handover note), then a 15-minute face-to-face with the manager.
2. Spend the notice period at your usual standard or better.
3. Decline counter-offers cleanly if you've decided; revisit nothing you decided twice.

**Pitfalls** — Accepting a counter-offer on the spot, then finding your replacement onboarded within weeks. Venting publicly. Treating the exit interview as therapy.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "My last day is Friday, 6 November 2026; handover notes attached. Thank you for the two years." Written first, spoken second, drama never.

### Firing or delivering bad news as the person in power

**Context** — You now hold the axe: terminating someone, closing a project, announcing a restructure. Power inverts all the normal rules: what feels honest to you feels sudden to them.

**Reading**
1. The person often already knows on some level; shock is usually about wording, not news.
2. HR presence, legality of what's said, and what goes into the record are as important as tone.
3. The room remembers how the news was delivered for years; the news itself fades faster.

**Response options**
1. Two sentences, then stop: "We made a decision to end this project. I want to explain why and what happens next." Don't pad, don't bury in context.
2. Give the person one run of questions before HR takes over.
3. Own the decision: passive voice ("it has been decided") reads as cowardice from a position of power.

**Pitfalls** — Citing performance gaps from a year the person never heard about. Ending with fake optimism ("great things ahead!"). Letting them hear it from a colleague first.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "As of today your role is being eliminated. That's a company decision I own. Let's walk through severance, dates, and what I can say as a reference."
