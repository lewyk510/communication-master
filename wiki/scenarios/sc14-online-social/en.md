# Online Social & Communities · English signals, decoded

Entries for forum threads, comment sections, group chats, and DMs as they actually read in Malaysian and international English-speaking spaces (Reddit, Lowyat, Discord, Facebook groups). Every example is a `constructed example` unless noted.

### "Don't feed the troll."

**Context** — A thread where one account is posting bait: deliberately wrong claims, personal jabs, or escalating provocations. Older members start replying with this phrase.

**Reading** — Three candidates: (1) a warning that the account has a trolling history and no argument to offer; (2) a hint that you, the responder, are now dominating the thread with angry replies and it looks bad; (3) a genuine observation that the thread has stopped producing information.

**Response options**
1. Take the hint and stop replying in that thread; post nothing further, even a final word.
2. Reply once, calmly, addressed to readers rather than the troll, then leave the thread for good.
3. Report the account to mods with links, especially if the bait targets a person.

**Pitfalls** — Treating "don't feed the troll" as itself an attack and arguing with the person who said it. That starts the exact flame spiral the phrase exists to prevent.

**Examples** — New account posts "EVs are a scam and everyone here knows it" in a car forum. You type three rebuttals, delete two, post one with a link, and never return to the thread. `constructed example`

### "Source?"

**Context** — A one-word or one-link reply demanding citations after you make a claim. Common on Reddit, Facebook debate groups, and quote-posts.

**Reading** — Three candidates: (1) genuine curiosity, wants to read more; (2) a stalling tactic, because no source will ever be accepted; (3) a signal to bystanders that your claim is unverified, whatever you answer.

**Response options**
1. Give the best single link, plus one line on what it shows. One link, not ten.
2. Answer with the source type if the exact link is not at hand: "Reported figure from 2023; I'll dig up the link when I'm at my desk."
3. If the thread has already turned hostile, note the source exists and stop: "It's in the article linked at the top of this thread."

**Pitfalls** — Flooding ten links as a flex. Nobody reads them and it reads as insecurity. Also, taking "Source?" from a bad-faith account as a promise of good-faith engagement; test with one reply before investing.

**Examples** — "Vaping helps smokers quit." "Source?" You post one Cochrane review link with "synthesis of RCTs; effect modest." He replies "cochrane lol shill." Thread over, your credibility with readers intact. `constructed example`

### Whoosh. (the missed irony)

**Context** — Someone takes a joke or obvious sarcasm literally and answers it seriously, often at length. Others reply "whoosh" (the sound of the joke going over their head).

**Reading** — Only one reading: the person missed the register switch. Whether the correction is cruel or kind depends on the community; some threads are just waiting to dogpile the person.

**Response options**
1. Let it pass. Pointing out "it was a joke" publicly adds humiliation without adding information.
2. Gentle rescue if you know them: "btw that first comment was sarcasm, the rest of what you said stands on its own."
3. Self-correction if you were the one who whooshed: "ha, missed the sarcasm entirely, moving on."

**Pitfalls** — Joining a whoosh pile-on. The person already looks bad; piling on costs you nothing now and buys goodwill nowhere. Also, over-explaining your own joke kills it twice.

**Examples** — Sarcastic post: "Yes, sure, corruption fixes itself." Long serious reply follows. Nobody replies to the serious reply; the thread simply moves on. `constructed example`

### Ratio'd (the quote-post pile-on)

**Context** — Your post gets more hostile replies or quote-posts than likes. The ratio itself becomes the story: "this post is getting ratio'd."

**Reading** — Two candidates: (1) you said something that genuinely offends a large group; (2) you got quoted by a big account and their audience flooded in without your original context.

**Reading the situation matters more than arguing.** PEN America's field manual interviews describe this exact experience from the target's side: "Suddenly I was getting 100 tweets a minute," with the hardest task being to tell real threats from noise.

**Response options**
1. Screenshot and archive everything first, including links and timestamps, before touching or deleting anything.
2. One calm reply addressed to the newcomers (context, link to your full statement), then lock: no further replies.
3. Go quiet: set DMs to closed, make the account private for a week, tell one offline friend what is happening.

**Pitfalls** — Deleting the original post early: it erases your own evidence and feeds the "they deleted it, guilty" story. Replying individually to a hundred accounts: impossible, and each reply is new screenshot material.

**Examples** — Your restaurant review gets quoted by a local influencer with "imagine saying this about a family business." You archive 60 screenshots, post one reply with the full paragraph in context, close DMs, and log off for the evening. `constructed example`

### "Check your DMs." (unsolicited DM etiquette)

**Context** — Someone slides into your DMs with no public interaction first: an offer, a request, a correction, or flirtation. Or a public thread ends with "DM me."

**Reading** — Three candidates: (1) legit but shy, wants privacy for a sensitive topic (salary, health, buying/selling); (2) someone escaping the public record to be ruder than they would be in the thread; (3) scam, sales, or recruitment funnel.

**Response options**
1. Ask for the agenda before engaging: "What's this about?" One question filters most of the risk.
2. Keep it public if the topic belongs in public: "Can you post it in the thread? Others might want the answer too."
3. Engage but redact: give ranges instead of exact figures, no addresses, no documents, nothing you would not want screenshotted.

**Pitfalls** — Treating a DM as private in any real sense. Anything you type can leave as a screenshot. And on the sending side: DMing a stranger without stating who you are and where you came from reads as either a scam or a threat, even when you mean well.

**Examples** — "Hi, saw your post in r/Malaysia about the apartment. I'm the current tenant moving out, happy to answer questions." That self-introduction in the first line is the whole difference between a welcome DM and a creepy one. `constructed example`

### Wall of text (the exhausting post)

**Context** — A reply of fifteen unbroken lines, no paragraphs, in a comment section built for three-line exchanges.

**Reading** — Two candidates: (1) the writer genuinely cares and lost all sense of reader budget; (2) the writer is burying a weak point in volume, so that refuting it takes more effort than writing it.

**Response options**
1. Quote one sentence, the load-bearing one, and reply only to that: "The claim that matters here is X, and here's the problem with it."
2. Ask for the short version: "TL;DR in one line?" This is legitimate on forums; it forces compression.
3. Skip it. If nobody else is engaging, your attention is not obligated.

**Pitfalls** — Matching the wall with a bigger wall. Two people writing essays at each other is precisely the flame war Virginia Shea described: a few people dominating a shared space with heat and no light, unfair to everyone reading.

**Examples** — Someone replies to your gear guide with 1,200 words on why you are wrong. You quote their final sentence and answer it in four lines. The thread's readers now have a focal point. `constructed example`

### "Removed: Rule 3." (when the mods act on you)

**Context** — Your post or comment disappears; a mod bot or human leaves a removal reason citing a numbered rule. Subreddit, Lowyat, and Discord all have versions of this.

**Reading** — Three candidates: (1) you actually broke the rule and misread it; (2) moderator discretion, the post was technically legal but unwelcome; (3) an automod false positive, extremely common with keyword filters.

**Response options**
1. Read the cited rule first, then decide. Most failed appeals come from people who never read it.
2. Modmail a short appeal: cite the rule, show how your post fits or offer to edit. No sarcasm, one message.
3. Accept the verdict and post elsewhere. A community is not a court; endless appeals cost you the account.

**Pitfalls** — Posting "why was this removed?? censorship" as a new thread. That meta-complaint almost always earns a second removal or a ban, and it burns the goodwill you would need for the actual appeal.

**Examples** — Your marketplace post is removed for a missing price. Modmail: "Rule 4 requires a price; I've added RM450 shipped. Could you re-approve?" Approved in an hour. `constructed example`

### "I'm just playing devil's advocate."

**Context** — Someone argues a controversial position in a thread about your identity, community, or livelihood, then claims neutrality with this phrase when challenged.

**Reading** — Three candidates: (1) a genuine, if clumsy, attempt to stress-test ideas; (2) a shield: advocating a position while collecting none of its social cost; (3) fishing for engagement or outrage.

**Response options**
1. Take it seriously once: respond to the strongest form of the argument, once, in writing, for the audience.
2. Name the asymmetry calmly: "Devil's advocate is fine, but you've made four claims in a row that all happen to hurt people in this thread."
3. Disengage from the person, not the topic: keep answering the argument if other, sincere people are asking it.

**Pitfalls** — Attacking the label instead of the argument. "No you're not, you're a troll" wins nothing with readers. Also, spending three hours arguing with someone who has declared in advance that none of it is theirs to defend.

**Examples** — "DA: maybe renters just shouldn't get pets?" You reply once: "Strongest case for your position is hygiene; here's the data on deposits instead. Anything beyond that is taste, not policy." Then you stop. `constructed example`

### "Can the mods lock this?" (de-escalation by structure)

**Context** — A thread you started, or one you are active in, is sliding: temperatures rising, personal digs appearing, regulars tagging mods.

**Reading** — Only one: the thread's information value is gone. What remains is a contest of endurance between two or three people, watched by a diminishing audience.

**Response options**
1. As OP, stop replying and ask for a lock: "Mods, this has run its course, please lock." Asking for the lock yourself reads as confidence, not defeat.
2. Post a closing summary for readers: "Where this landed: X is true, Y is contested, Z is my error, thanks for the corrections." Readers remember the summary, not the fight.
3. Walk out without a summary if the thread has gone personal; your last post being a jab is a screenshot that outlives the thread.

**Pitfalls** — The "one last reply" instinct. There is no last reply in a flame war, only a later one. Also, locking a thread where you are losing but the conversation is still productive; that reads as hiding.

**Examples** — Your gaming-community thread about balance patches turns into two people trading insults through quote-replies. You post the three-line summary, then DM a mod. Thread locked within the hour, both regulars cool off, your summary is the last visible post. `constructed example`
