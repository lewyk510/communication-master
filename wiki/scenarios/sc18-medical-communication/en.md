# Medical Communication · English Signal Handbook

Written from the English-speaking patient's chair: the three-minute consult, shared decision-making, pain vocabulary, caregiver advocacy, and serious-news conversations. Each entry stands alone; not a translation of the other lanes. **This is communication guidance, not medical advice — no diagnosis or treatment instructions here.**

### "So, what brings you in today?" — the one-sentence opener

**Context** — the universal first question in any clinic, from a private GP to a government polyclinic. It sets the search direction for the whole consult.

**Reading**
1. The doctor wants a locatable chief complaint: site + character + duration.
2. A compressed opener buys you time — every minute not spent extracting your history is a minute for your questions.
3. A vague opener ("I've just been feeling off") turns the consult into guesswork.

**Response options**
1. Complaint sentence: "Left knee pain for three weeks, worse on stairs."
2. Curve sentence: "Ice helps; it's worst when I stand up after sitting."
3. Context sentence: "I've tried paracetamol with limited effect; I have hypertension and I'm allergic to penicillin."

**Pitfalls** — narrating your medical autobiography from three months ago; listing eight symptoms at once; opening with your internet self-diagnosis.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Chest tightness for two days, comes on climbing stairs, settles after a few minutes' rest — my father had a stent at sixty." The doctor says "that's exactly what I needed," and moves straight to examination.

### "What are my options?" — the four-question toolkit

**Context** — the moment a plan is proposed. Most patients nod and leave; this is the highest-density free question window in the room.

**Reading**
1. The question is legitimate and encouraged: the UK Patients Association frames shared decision-making around four questions — benefits? risks? alternatives? what happens if I do nothing? (Evidence-backed, see [patients-assoc-4q].)
2. NHS England's "Ask 3 Questions" campaign pushes the same move: options, pros and cons, support for deciding (Evidence-backed, see [search-ask-doctor].)
3. "Doing nothing" is a real option — asking about it instantly distinguishes watchful waiting from over-treatment.

**Response options**
1. "What are my options here — including monitoring instead of treating?"
2. "What are the benefits and risks of each, roughly how often do the risks happen?"
3. "If I choose to do nothing for now, what would happen?"

**Pitfalls** — staying silent to avoid seeming to challenge the doctor; asking questions you don't stay to hear the answers to; debating with articles instead of asking.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "What are my options?" — "Surgery or physiotherapy." — "What happens if I wait three months?" The doctor visibly relaxes and walks through all three paths with numbers.

### Pain words that give doctors traction

**Context** — "How would you describe the pain?" stalls almost everyone. English has a precise working vocabulary; using it saves three rounds of follow-up questions.

**Reading**
1. Descriptors narrow the differential for the doctor — you are only responsible for translating sensation accurately, not for interpreting it.
2. "It really hurts" carries zero information; "like a needle, 6 out of 10" carries four dimensions.
3. Healthify NZ's clinician questions are exactly three: how long, how bad, what makes it better or worse (Evidence-backed, see [healthify-pain].)

**Response options**
1. Quality + number: "Sharp and burning, about 6 out of 10, 8 at worst."
2. Triggers: "Worst at night when I roll over; walking barely hurts."
3. Function sentence: "It wakes me twice a night and I can't climb stairs." — often says more than the number.

**Pitfalls** — stoicism ("I can manage") that under-codes severity; inflating everything to 10/10, which breaks calibration; numbers without context.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Throbbing and cramping, comes in waves, 5–6 out of 10, worse after meals." One sentence delivers quality, rhythm, intensity, and trigger.

### Medication questions at prescription and pickup

**Context** — after the prescription is written and again at the pharmacy counter. Two checkpoints, one question set.

**Reading**
1. AHRQ's patient sheet lists the questions verbatim: What is the medicine for? How am I supposed to take it and for how long? What side effects are likely, and what do I do if they occur? Is it safe with my other medicines and supplements? (Evidence-backed, see [ahrq-20tips].)
2. Label confusion is a named risk: "four times daily" may mean every 6 hours around the clock or just waking hours — AHRQ's own example (Evidence-backed, see [ahrq-20tips].)
3. "Brown bagging" — bringing every medicine, vitamin, and supplement you take to the visit — is AHRQ's tool for catching interactions (Evidence-backed, see [ahrq-20tips].)

**Response options**
1. At the desk: "What's this for, how do I take it, and what should I do if I get side effects?"
2. At the counter: "Is this the medicine my doctor prescribed?" (AHRQ's exact pickup check.)
3. Written backup: "Can I have written information about side effects — and which symptoms mean I should call you?"

**Pitfalls** — asking the pharmacist nothing because "the doctor already told me"; measuring liquid medicine with a household teaspoon (AHRQ: use a marked device); deciding on your own to swap to a cheaper medicine — ask, don't switch.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Before I go — is this safe with the fish oil I take?" The pharmacist checks the system and adjusts the timing, preventing a weeks-long interaction.

### Teach-back: "So I've got this right…"

**Context** — the final 60 seconds before you stand up. The most missing move in the English-language consult: everyone says "got it," nobody checks.

**Reading**
1. "Understood?" "Yes" is politeness, not comprehension — most "yes"es evaporate by the car park.
2. Restating converts one-way telling into two-way verification and hands the doctor a zero-cost error-correction moment.
3. AHRQ frames clear discharge instructions as the thing that prevents an unexpected return trip to hospital (Evidence-backed, see [ahrq-20tips].)

**Response options**
1. "Let me play this back: one tablet morning and night, after food, for two weeks, then a blood test — correct?"
2. "So the plan is try this for two weeks, and if it's not better, we do the scan?"
3. "Who contacts me with the results, and by when?" — AHRQ: don't assume no news is good news (Evidence-backed, see [ahrq-20tips].)

**Pitfalls** — restating as an argument instead of a check; recording only the parts you like; restating without writing it down immediately.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "So one in the morning, two at night?" — "Good catch, one at night." A single teach-back catches a dosing ambiguity before it becomes a week of wrong doses.

### Asking for a second opinion without burning the bridge

**Context** — before a big decision: surgery, long-term medication, a serious diagnosis. The fear of offending the current doctor keeps most people from ever asking.

**Reading**
1. Second opinions are medically normal: doctors genuinely differ where evidence is unclear, and most welcome the request (Evidence-backed, see [msd-second-opinion].)
2. The second doctor should not be the first doctor's close associate — perspectives overlap (Evidence-backed, see [msd-second-opinion].)
3. Have records and images sent ahead so you don't repeat tests (Evidence-backed, see [msd-second-opinion].)

**Response options**
1. "I'd like another opinion before deciding — could you help with a referral and copies of my results?"
2. "This is a big decision; I want to discuss it with my family and consult one more specialist. Does that sound reasonable?"
3. In public-system terms: "Could you refer me on for a specialist review before we proceed?"

**Pitfalls** — going behind the doctor's back with incomplete records on both sides; choosing a second opinion from the first doctor's business partner; framing the request as an accusation.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Could I get a second opinion before the surgery? Could you give me a copy of my scans?" — "Of course, I'll burn the images to a disc and write the referral." Ten minutes, relationship intact.

### Advocating for an elderly parent: let them answer

**Context** — accompanying an aging parent to the clinic. The classic failure: the visit becomes a two-way conversation between you and the doctor while the patient watches.

**Reading**
1. NIA's caregiver guidance is explicit: let the older adult answer unless asked, and don't turn the visit into a two-way conversation — always include the person (Evidence-backed, see [nia-caregiver].)
2. Your real job is threefold: bring the lists (medicines + questions), take notes, debrief afterwards — not answer for them.
3. "There's nothing much wrong, really" may be cost-avoidance or pride; ask privately beforehand what they most want to raise.

**Response options**
1. Hand over gracefully: "Mom has three questions written down — she'll ask them herself; I'll fill in gaps."
2. Notes with permission: "May I note the medicine instructions so we can follow them at home?"
3. Close the loop: "Could we have the written instructions, and when is the follow-up?" (NIA's recommended ask — Evidence-backed, see [nia-caregiver].)

**Pitfalls** — answering "she's just a bit tired" (fainting language magnified by your translation); reciting the medicine list from memory instead of bringing the boxes; skipping the same-evening debrief.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: A son slides a handwritten question list to the doctor: "Her eyesight's poor — I'll read them out and she picks the order." The doctor: "That's the best-prepared list I've seen today."

### Requesting an interpreter or dialect support

**Context** — when the patient's strongest language isn't the clinic's working language — common with dialect-speaking elders in multilingual systems.

**Reading**
1. It is a routine request, not a special favour: staff handle multilingual patients daily.
2. Breakage happens in the details — dosing, side effects, follow-up timing — precisely where errors hurt most.
3. Family members can interpret conversation, but medical terms should still be confirmed with staff; a mistranslated dose has no error-correction loop.

**Response options**
1. "My father is most comfortable in Hokkien — could a colleague help interpret, or should we arrange one for the next visit?"
2. Double-check protocol: "Could you explain it once more while I confirm he's understood the dose correctly?"
3. Written anchor: "Could the key instructions be written down so we can translate them at home?"

**Pitfalls** — assuming "he understands enough English"; using a young grandchild as the interpreter for dosage talk; translating medical terms loosely ("a bit of blood pressure medicine") instead of by name.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Before we start — could we have a Mandarin speaker join, even by phone?" The clinic calls a bilingual nurse; the consult proceeds with the doctor's sentences relayed and the dose written on paper in both languages.

### Receiving serious news: questions that anchor you

**Context** — diagnosis conversations, abnormal results, disease changes. Under shock, working memory collapses; structure replaces courage.

**Reading**
1. SPIKES requires clinicians to check understanding and give information in small chunks — you can co-drive that structure instead of being run over by it (Evidence-backed, see [spikes-mdanderson].)
2. Patients with a clear plan for the future are less anxious — the protocol's own words (Evidence-backed, see [spikes-mdanderson].)
3. Details heard in the room rarely survive to the car park: notes and restatement beat memory.

**Response options**
1. Pace it: "Please go step by step — I'm going to take notes."
2. Calibrate: "What I'm hearing is X — is that right?"
3. Move forward: "What are the next steps, and when will we know more?"

**Pitfalls** — making a major decision in the room unless it's urgent (ask for a follow-up with family present); reading a struggling clinician as coldness (in the original survey, 52% of oncologists found empathic response the hardest step — Evidence-backed, see [spikes-mdanderson].); leaving without writing down anything.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "I'm going to repeat this back so I know I've understood — then can we talk about what happens next?" The doctor slows down, re-segments, and the patient leaves with a written next-steps list instead of a fog.

### Closing the loop: red flags and follow-up instructions

**Context** — the last two minutes of any visit, the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.

**Reading**
1. "Come back if anything's wrong" is a zero-instruction template; asking converts it into a concrete list.
2. The answer also tells you what does not need panic — the inverse is your reassurance list.
3. Asking who chases the test results prevents the silent-lab-result failure mode (AHRQ's "don't assume no news is good news" — Evidence-backed, see [ahrq-20tips].)

**Response options**
1. "What specific symptoms mean I should come back immediately or go to A&E?"
2. Quantify the boundary: "What fever, for how many days, would worry you?"
3. Post-discharge (AHRQ's list): "Do I keep taking my old medicines? When is follow-up? When can I return to normal activity?" (Evidence-backed, see [ahrq-20tips].)

**Pitfalls** — hearing the red-flag list without writing it down; treating the red flags as the only threshold (any change that worries you is worth a call); forgetting to ask who delivers the results.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "If the wound gets redder, starts discharging, or I run a fever over 38°C — same day, come back." Day three: discharge noticed; red flag triggered; same-evening review.
