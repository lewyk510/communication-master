# Job Interviews · English Signals Handbook

Written from the English-speaking interview room: HR screens, hiring-manager rounds, panels, and video calls. Each entry stands alone; not a translation of the other lanes.

### "Tell me about yourself."

**Context** — The universal opener, usually seconds after you sit down. Attention is at its peak and the interviewer is skimming your resume.

**Reading**
1. Literal: a verbal resume summary, a chance to verify your documents.
2. Scoring: do you carry a 60-second structure (who I am, what proves it, why here)?
3. Head down means they are hunting for resume gaps; head up means content scoring has started.

**Response options**
1. Present-past-future: one sentence on where you are now, two proof points, one sentence bridging to this role.
2. Never recite your resume verbatim; add one motive or detail that is not on the page.
3. Leave an intentional hook so the follow-up question lands in your strongest territory.

**Pitfalls** — A life-story tour; going past 90 seconds; ending with "so, yeah, that's about it," which hands the floor back with nothing gained.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "I run supply chain for a cross-border e-commerce brand; in two years I cut our stockout rate from 18% to 4%. The interesting part was a forecasting mistake we caught early — happy to walk you through it." The interviewer takes the hook.

### "Tell me about a time when..." (behavioural questions)

**Context** — The signature of behavioural and competency interviews. Openers: "Tell me about a time when...", "Give me an example of when you...".

**Reading**
1. They want evidence of past behaviour, not hypotheticals; "I would usually..." starts losing points immediately.
2. Each question maps to a competency tag (resilience, conflict, leadership); naming the tag beats telling a random story.
3. Probing ("what exactly did you do?") is a authenticity check — the story must survive detail.

**Response options**
1. Deliver STAR: about 15 seconds of context, one line of task, 70% on your own actions, a result with a number.
2. Prepare a small library of situations and re-angle the same story for pressure, failure, and conflict questions.
3. If you lack direct experience, say so and transfer: "I haven't faced that exact case, but the closest mechanism I've run is..."

**Pitfalls** — Telling a team story with no "I"; unquantified results; stories over three minutes.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: Asked for a failure, a candidate describes a launch that slipped three days, then lands on "the upstream review process I built afterwards" — and scores top marks on the growth competency.

### "What is your greatest weakness?"

**Context** — Near-mandatory. Both HR and hiring managers use it to test self-awareness, not to collect confessions.

**Reading**
1. A candour test: do they want honesty, or will you dodge with "I'm a perfectionist"?
2. A fit check: does your weakness collide with the role's core KPI?
3. A growth check: is your remediation real and evidenced?

**Response options**
1. Real, non-fatal, in repair: "I can over-polish details and slow delivery; I now lock an 80% version against a priority list first."
2. Attach an action, not an adjective: follow the weakness with one concrete step you took last quarter.
3. If the weakness IS the job, own it with a compensation structure: "Analytics is my weaker side; on my team I partner with X for that layer."

**Pitfalls** — Fake weaknesses spotted in one second; fatal admissions ("I don't like numbers" for an accounting role); admitting with no repair plan, which reads as no growth.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Public presentations used to freeze me. My fix: I rehearse any big delivery with two colleagues at least three times. It worked — that's partly why I'm comfortable doing product demos now."

### "Why are you leaving your current job?"

**Context** — Standard for any switcher, and the most delicate question in the room: pull versus push.

**Reading**
1. Loyalty probe: would you speak about us this way someday?
2. Consistency check: does your story match references and your LinkedIn trail?
3. Pull test: they are listening for what you are running toward, not what you are escaping.

**Response options**
1. Reframe with respect: one graceful line on the old employer, then pivot to what you are seeking.
2. Align with the new role's vocabulary so the listener mentally casts you in the seat.
3. Neutral facts (relocation, restructuring, product line sunset) stated plainly, with no private grievances.

**Pitfalls** — Venting about salary or a "toxic" boss; "I was bored"; inconsistent versions caught across rounds by different interviewers.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "My previous company gave me a lot, but after the reorganisation my function stopped having a roadmap. I want somewhere I can build for years — which is why this role caught my eye."

### "What are your salary expectations?"

**Context** — Usually HR screening, sometimes the final conversation. It is a filter, not (yet) a negotiation.

**Reading**
1. Budget fitting: outside their band, you get screened out regardless of fit.
2. Market research test: how you quote reveals whether you did your homework.
3. "That's a bit above our band" can be a real constraint or an opening gambit to shave your number — listen for whether a counter-offer path is offered.

**Response options**
1. Bounce with a question: "Could you share the budgeted range for the role?" and position yourself once you hear it.
2. Quote a researched range with your floor as the low end: "Market data I've seen puts this between RM 7–9k; I'd be comfortable in the mid-range."
3. Early-round deferral: "I'd like to understand the full scope before discussing comp." In a final round this deferral reads as evasive — give a number.

**Pitfalls** — Revealing your floor as your opener in round one; a single point (no room to agree); numbers detached from level and market,

**Examples** — `constructed example`: The candidate answers "I've seen the band for this role locally is 7–9k; around 8 fits me." HR counters "budget is 7.5." The range answer is what made the half-band of negotiation room visible.

### "Do you have any questions for us?"

**Context** — The closing beat of almost every interview. Many candidates wave it off; it is actually the last scored item.

**Reading**
1. A thermometer: no questions equals questionable interest.
2. Layer check: whose seatinterviewer you face (HR, line manager, executive) decides which layer of question earns most.
3. A power transfer: the only stretch of the hour you control, and the room usually relaxes into it.

**Response options**
1. Two questions minimum, matched to seat: to HR ask process ("what does success look like in the first 90 days?"); to executives ask strategy ("what's the biggest problem this business line must solve this year?").
2. Convert something they said: "You mentioned the team is restructuring — would I inherit an existing book or open a new one?"
3. End by locking the loop: "What are the next steps and your timeline?" — which creates your legitimate follow-up hook.

**Pitfalls** — "No, I think you covered everything" (you dropped the last bonus question); first-round questions about leave and OT (save those for offer stage); "So what does your company actually do?" (instant disqualification).

**Examples** — `constructed example`: A candidate asks, "What's the thing you personally enjoy most about leading this team?" The director talks for two minutes, visibly warmer, and the final round converts.

### Silence, interruptions, and the note-taking freeze

**Context** — Live or on video: the interviewer goes quiet, freezes while typing, or overlaps your answer.

**Reading**
1. Silence usually means notes, not disinterest — say so aloud so it can't be misread.
2. Talking over you is often latency, not rudeness; restore turn-taking rather than racing.
3. A dropped call is recoverable in your favour — calm, fast, no complaints.

**Response options**
1. Narrate pauses: "I'm just jotting a note here — please go on," and check in if silence runs past a few seconds.
2. On overlap, yield gracefully: "Go ahead — I'll pause after this sentence."
3. After a reconnect, re-anchor the thread: "Sorry, that was my connection. We were at the part about X — shall I continue?"

**Pitfalls** — Watching yourself instead of the camera; an unmuted mic broadcasting your keyboard; blaming their platform audibly.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: The interviewer's video freezes for ten seconds. The candidate drops into chat: "You might have frozen on your side — I'll wait." On reconnection the interviewer notes, "You handled that well."

### Negotiating an offer: paper first, multi-issue always

**Context** — The verbal offer call: promises fly, details lurk. This stage decides your pay for year one and sets the tone of the relationship.

**Reading**
1. "You're happy with the salary then?" is pressure to accept in the moment; don't take that hand.
2. "That's our final number" may be a true base constraint while signing bonus, title, leave, or review timing still move.
3. "Take your time" means the window is open — a same-day yes usually leaves money on the table.

**Response options**
1. Paper everything: request the written offer with full compensation structure before negotiating anything.
2. Negotiate multi-issue: when base is capped, trade across signing bonus, review timing, WFH days, learning budget, title — two or three at a time, framed as building the package together.
3. Justify every counter with market bands, scarce skills, or a competing offer — reasons, not feelings.

**Pitfalls** — Asking "is this offer negotiable?" ( Harvard's Program on Negotiation advice: never request permission — counter directly); pushing back with no justification; accepting, then reversing days later, which burns the relationship.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: HR says base is capped; the candidate replies, "Understood on base — could we look at a signing bonus or a six-month salary review instead?" The final offer includes the review clause.

### Acting as the interviewer: structure beats instinct

**Context** — You are on the other side of the table, running campus or lateral interviews with hiring stakes.

**Reading**
1. Dana, Dawes & Peterson (2013) showed unstructured interviews can *worsen* accuracy (dilution) while inflating confidence (sensemaking): your "feel" for a candidate is the bias, not the signal.
2. A confident impression can be built even from random noise; only recorded behavioural evidence separates it from luck.
3. Whom you warm to in the first minutes predicts your scoring unless you pre-commit to anchors.

**Response options**
1. Anchor first: fix your questions and rating anchors before the interview, score each answer independently, decide late.
2. Score behaviour, not vibes: write down what the candidate actually did ("built the model; verified with two clients") rather than impressions ("seems sharp").
3. Separate first impressions from conclusions: note both, revisit notes after all interviews, and compare against other interviewers' evidence before group debriefs.

**Pitfalls** — Deciding in the first five minutes and spending the rest confirming; letting one dazzling answer wash out three mediocre ones (the dilution trap in reverse); skipping note-writing and trusting memory after five back-to-back candidates.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: An interviewer initially dislikes a candidate's terse style but has committed to scoring each answer on evidence; the notes later show the candidate gave the only answers quantifying risk — and scores highest.
