# Teaching & Coaching · English register

Signals and responses for the English-speaking lane: one-on-ones, code review, tutoring, parenting, online teaching. Written natively for how these moments actually sound in English.

### "Does that make sense?"

**Context** — The default comprehension check at the end of any explanation: meetings, tutoring, a senior walking a junior through a design.

**Reading**
1. Politeness ritual: the expected answer is "yes," not the truth.
2. Genuine uncertainty by the explainer about their own clarity, with no better tool at hand.
3. Status move (rare): a boss's "make sense?" that forecloses questions.

**Response options**
1. As the listener, convert it into a teach-back: "Mostly. Let me play it back and you correct me."
2. As the explainer, replace it permanently: "Tell me one case where you'd use this."
3. With kids: "Show me how it works. Teach me."

**Pitfalls** — This question measures face, not understanding. Saying "no" costs more socially than nodding, so it returns "yes" at near-100% rates regardless of what actually landed.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: Three "make sense?" in a row, three nods, and the confusion surfaces as a production bug two weeks later. One teach-back on the spot would have caught it in thirty seconds.

### "Actually, it's really simple"

**Context** — An explainer's opener, meant to lower the listener's guard.

**Reading**
1. Kind intent: don't be scared of this.
2. Curse of knowledge speaking: simple *for me*, brand new for you.
3. A setup for impatience: "it's simple" quietly becomes "so your confusion is your fault."

**Response options**
1. As the explainer, swap the frame: "This took me two days to get when I first learned it. Let's take it slow."
2. As the listener, recalibrate: "New to me. Start from zero."
3. With children, ban the word and say "this takes a few tries" instead.

**Pitfalls** — "Simple" is a relative word, and the moment you say it, it grades the listener. It's half a step from "you should've gotten this already."

**Examples** — `Practice heuristic`: swap openers: "it's easy" → "there's a pattern"; "really simple" → "three steps"; "you should know this" → "let me check what you've seen before."

### "Walk me through your thinking"

**Context** — The coach's opening move when someone brings a problem, a bug, or a draft. Also the standard Socratic entry point.

**Reading**
1. Genuine interest in the reasoning, so the fix targets the source, not the symptom.
2. A deliberate refusal to hand over the answer.
3. Rarely, a preamble to rebuttal ("walk me through... that's just wrong"). Tone decides.

**Response options**
1. Answer, then hand the question back: "I'm stuck between A and B. Which should I test first?"
2. As the asker, then shut up. Count to ten; silence is thinking, not a lull.
3. If they say "no idea," drop a level: "What have you already ruled out?"

**Pitfalls** — The fake version: asking, then talking over the answer with your own solution. The learner concludes their reasoning is unwelcome, and next time they bring you the symptom instead of the problem.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: A junior reports an error; the senior only says "read three lines above the stack trace and tell me what's happening there." The junior spots the cause unaided. Retrieval beats being told.

### "Here's what I saw, and here's the impact"

**Context** — Developmental feedback using CCL's SBI shape: name the Situation, describe the observable Behavior, state the Impact.

**Reading**
1. Structured, non-judgmental feedback: facts only, no adjectives about the person.
2. A deliberate contrast with "you always..." character reviews.
3. If followed by "what was your thinking?", it's the full SBI(I): a two-way intent probe.

**Response options**
1. Listen, then supply context if asked: "Yes, and here's what was happening on my end."
2. As the giver, keep B pure: "In today's review, you interrupted twice while I presented slides" (not "you're rude").
3. Close the loop with intent: "What was going through your mind at the time?"

**Pitfalls** — The model breaks at B. The instant you say "your attitude," you've left observable behavior and handed the listener a defense to build. Keep it to what a camera would have recorded.

**Examples** — `Evidence-backed`: CCL recommends pairing the Impact statement with an inquiry about intent, turning one-way feedback into a feedback conversation where intent vs. impact can be clarified (see Sources).

### "What would you try first?"

**Context** — GROW's Options and Will stages compressed to a sentence: a coachee has talked through Goal and Reality, and the temptation to hand them your answer is peaking.

**Reading**
1. Genuine coaching stance: their plan, their ownership.
2. A calibration check: how far did they already get before asking.
3. A gentle push: this one is yours to carry.

**Response options**
1. Offer options with a lean: "I see two paths, A is fast but fragile, B is slow but safe. I lean B. What do you think?"
2. If they freeze, return to Reality: "What do we know for sure right now?"
3. As the coach, always close with a timestamp: "Okay, B. What's the first step, and when?"

**Pitfalls** — Options without a Will close is a pleasant chat. No owner, no date, no first step means nothing gets encoded in memory.

**Examples** — `Practice heuristic`: the fifteen-minute GROW: "What do you want? Where are you now? What's the first thing you'll do, and when?" The third question must land on a calendar date.

### "You did X well, because..."

**Context** — Specific praise, the counterweight to vague "good job." Works for juniors, kids, and peers.

**Reading**
1. Real recognition, anchored to a behavior so it can be repeated.
2. Signal of attention: you noticed the work, not just the deadline.
3. Deliberate effort-praise over talent-praise (for children especially).

**Response options**
1. Accept it plainly: "Thanks, that took a few rewrites." Don't deflect.
2. As the giver, name the mechanism: "You caught the edge case before I asked. That's exactly what saves us rework."
3. With kids: praise the path, "you split the problem in half by yourself," not the label, "you're so smart."

**Pitfalls** — Vague praise ("great work!") has no learning value and, when it always precedes a "but," becomes a warning shot that devalues all future praise.

**Examples** — `Practice heuristic`: a praise passes the bar if it names a behavior the person could repeat tomorrow. If you can't name one, it's mood, not feedback.

### "Let me try that again, differently"

**Context** — The explainer owns a failed explanation: the listener is lost, the same words have landed twice, frustration is starting on both sides.

**Reading**
1. Responsibility flip: "if you don't get it, that's on my delivery."
2. A de-escalation: it removes the listener's fear of being the problem.
3. A signal to change modality, not volume: diagram, example, or hands-on instead of more words.

**Response options**
1. Swap modality: "Forget the slides, let's do it on the whiteboard."
2. Shrink the chunk: "Let's just do step two and stop there."
3. As the listener, ask for the swap: "Can you show me instead of telling me?"

**Pitfalls** — The common failure is repeating the same sentence louder. If it didn't land the first time, the fix is a different route, not more decibels.

**Examples** — `Practice heuristic`: after two failed explanations, mandatory action: the learner does it while you watch. Watching the hands find the stuck step faster than any amount of re-telling.

### "Next time, try..."

**Context** — Feedforward phrasing (Goldsmith): point the advice at the future, after the present failure is already handled. Common in retros, post-mortems, and coaching kids after a bad game.

**Reading**
1. The failure is closed; this sentence is equipment for the next round.
2. Trust: you'll be doing this again, and you'll do it better.
3. A turn of the page, said out loud.

**Response options**
1. Full form: "Next time a client gets heated, don't explain first. Repeat their concern back."
2. Hand it over: "Next time this happens, what will you try first?"
3. Pre-brief version: "This client is tough. One thing to remember: the anger is at the proposal, not at you."

**Pitfalls** — Feedforward before the current mistake is actually processed reads as brushing it off. Close this round first (facts, ownership), then arm the next one.

**Examples** — `Practice heuristic`: end every correction with one of three sentences: "next time, start with...", "the lesson in one line is...", or "what's the first thing you'll do when you're back at your desk?" No closing sentence, no closed loop.

### "Take your time, we'll get there"

**Context** — A defensive or anxious learner: they froze, apologized too much, or pre-emptively called themselves stupid. Common with new hires and with kids facing a hard subject.

**Reading**
1. De-escalation first, content later: emotion is running the room.
2. Normalization: "everyone gets stuck here" removes the fear that struggling is evidence against them.
3. A pace reset, not a lower standard.

**Response options**
1. Normalize, then shrink the step: "Everyone trips on this one. Let's just do the first half."
2. Hand back agency: "Where do you want to start?"
3. If defensiveness is hot, stop teaching: "No rush. Let's just make sure I haven't misunderstood your thinking."

**Pitfalls** — Rushing past the emotion to deliver more content means none of the content arrives. Until the emotion is cleared, instruction doesn't stick.

**Examples** — `Practice heuristic`: correction cost ranking: specific behavior feedback beats pattern reminders; character commentary is banned. When someone is already defensive: slow down, then ask for their view.
