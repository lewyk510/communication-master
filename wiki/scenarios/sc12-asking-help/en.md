# Asking for Help · English Signals Playbook

Native English phrasing for the asking scenarios: the opener, the specific ask, the cold email, the follow-up. Each entry stands on its own; not translated from the zh or ms lanes.

### "Can you do me a favor?"

**Context** — The classic opener to a colleague or friend, spoken or by chat, before the actual request. Neutral stakes, relationship usually warm.

**Reading**
1. A small, genuine ask is coming; the opener is just a runway.
2. A bigger ask is coming, and the opener lowers your guard before it lands.
3. The person hasn't fully figured out what they need yet.

**Response options**
1. Skip the runway politely: "Sure, what do you need?" This collapses two turns into one.
2. Price it first: "Maybe. What's involved?" Asking the cost before saying yes is fair, not rude.
3. If your plate is full: "Depends on what it is, I'm slammed today. Tell me and I'll say straight if I can."

**Pitfalls** — Saying yes to the opener and then discovering it's a five-hour task; backing out after "sure" costs more trust than asking the price upfront.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Can you do me a favor?" "Sure, what's up?" "Can you proofread two pages by Thursday?" vs. the same opener followed by "rewrite my entire resume by tonight". The opener hid the true size in both, but only one was fair.

### The specific, time-boxed ask

**Context** — The core skill: a request with a concrete deliverable, a concrete deadline, and an explicit out. Chat or email, colleague or acquaintance.

**Reading**
1. A well-formed request signals the asker did their homework and respects your time.
2. Very specific asks can read as presumptuous if the relationship is thin ("review my whole deck by 9am").
3. An explicit out ("if not, no worries") reads as confidence, not weakness; helpers say they're *more* likely to help when asked directly, per Flynn & Lake's research.

**Response options**
1. If you can't commit fully, offer a slice: "Can't do the whole thing, but I can read pages 1–3 tonight."
2. If the timing is wrong, name the right window: "Thursday's bad, Friday morning I could."
3. If you must decline, decline cleanly: "I can't take this one on." A vague maybe is the worst gift.

**Pitfalls** — Burying the ask in three paragraphs of apology; asking so vaguely ("help with my project sometime?") that the helper has to design the task for you.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Could you sanity-check the numbers in section 2? Ten minutes tops, needed by Friday noon. If this week's bad, just say so and I'll ask Priya." Every element is present: task, cost, deadline, out.

### "Sorry to bother you" (and other apology overkill)

**Context** — Email or chat openers used, usually by juniors or conscientious people, before a perfectly legitimate request.

**Reading**
1. Simple politeness; harmless once.
2. Repeated every message, it trains the reader to treat your requests as nuisances.
3. It sometimes signals the person was scolded for asking before.

**Response options**
1. When receiving, cut the loop kindly: "You're never bothering me. What do you need?"
2. When sending, replace the apology with the reason: "Quick one because you built the pipeline: can you check one flag?"
3. Save the apology for actual imposition (weekend pings, third follow-up), where it does real work.

**Pitfalls** — An apology without a reason reads as asking permission to exist; one "sorry" per request is plenty.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Sorry to bother you, sorry, quick question sorry" (reader's attention already drained) vs. "Quick question: is the staging API rate-limited?" The second respects both sides.

### The cold ask (emailing a stranger)

**Context** — Requesting help from someone you don't know: a professor, an author, a hiring contact. Email or LinkedIn, one shot, no relationship yet.

**Reading**
1. Strangers agree more often than askers predict (Flynn & Lake found up to 50% underestimation), so the cold ask is worth sending.
2. But the stranger's yes depends almost entirely on the cost being visible and small.
3. Generic flattery up front ("I love your work!!") reads as template and lowers trust.

**Response options**
1. Front-load the ask in the first two lines: who you are (one clause), what you want (one sentence).
2. Make the cost concrete and small: "Would you answer three questions about X? 200 words or less."
3. Give the out explicitly: "If this isn't your area or your bandwidth, I completely understand."

**Pitfalls** — Three paragraphs of life story before the request; asking for "a chat sometime" (unbounded cost) instead of a bounded task; no follow-up after silence (one polite nudge after a week is normal, not rude).

**Examples** — `constructed example`: Subject "3 quick questions on your 2024 paper?" Body: "I'm a final-year student testing your method on river data. Could I ask you three short questions by email? If not, no problem at all and thanks for reading."

### Asking your manager: bring options, not just the problem

**Context** — Requesting help, a decision, or resources from a boss. Spoken in a 1:1 or by message; stakes high, evaluation context.

**Reading** (manager's side)
1. A bare "I'm stuck" reads as capability risk.
2. A request with options attached reads as ownership.
3. Late escalation (after the deadline broke) reads as the worst of the three, regardless of quality.

**Response options**
1. Three-beat structure: situation, what you tried, the decision you need ("I'm blocked on X, tried A and B, I recommend C and need your sign-off").
2. Offer a choice, not a question: "We can ship Friday with a patch Monday, or slip to Tuesday. Which risk do you prefer?"
3. If genuinely out of your depth, say so early, not at the deadline: "This is past my experience; I'd like an hour of Sam's time."

**Pitfalls** — "What should I do?" with no options attached; sitting on a growing problem because asking feels like admitting failure; asking publicly in a channel where the boss must answer for everyone to see.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Vendor's holding the export (situation). I chased twice and re-sent the spec (tried). We can wait for their fix, due Thursday, or pay for the rush API (options). I'd pay the rush; you're the approver (decision)."

### "Let me know if there's anything I can do"

**Context** — Said by a bystander to someone clearly overloaded (new parent, sick colleague, teammate in crisis). Spoken, or in a condolence message.

**Reading**
1. Genuine warmth, but it moves the work back onto the person drowning: now *they* must inventory tasks, choose one, and ask.
2. A placeholder for real help when the speaker doesn't know what helps.
3. Sometimes just social noise, equivalent to "thinking of you".

**Response options**
1. When receiving it and you need help, take it literally: "Actually yes, could you pick up the kids Tuesday?" Direct asks work better than hope, per the research on underestimating compliance.
2. When receiving it and you don't, a graceful hold: "That means a lot. I'll tell you if something comes up."
3. When *sending* it, upgrade it to an offer with options: "I can bring dinner Tuesday or walk the dog this week, which is more useful?" Choose for them.

**Pitfalls** — Saying it and then hoping you won't be taken up on it; making the drowning person manage your kindness.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: After a teammate's emergency, the person who said "I've got your standup notes covered this week" did more than three people who said "let me know if you need anything".

### The polite follow-up after a "no" (ask and ask again)

**Context** — Your first request was declined; the same person, some time later. Research by Newark, Bohns and Flynn (reported by Stanford GSB) found the yes-rate on a *second* request of the same size was *higher* than the first, because a second "no" costs the helper more discomfort than the first.

**Reading**
1. A "no" usually means "not now" or "not this", not "never".
2. The asker over-reads the first no as a verdict on the person's helpfulness.
3. Used badly, a follow-up becomes pressure; the line is one follow-up, with new information or a new frame.

**Response options**
1. Book the retry: "No problem. Could I check back next week if things ease up?" This converts no into "not now".
2. Retry with a smaller ask: "Totally fine. Would ten minutes on just page 2 work instead?"
3. Accept the second no and stop: "Understood, I'll sort it another way. Thanks for being straight with me."

**Pitfalls** — Reading the first no as hostility and vanishing (the paradox: we avoid the people most likely to help); or following up twice, which flips persistence into pestering.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: First ask (2 hours of review) declined. Two weeks later: "You said last time was slammed. It's genuinely 15 minutes now, one section, deadline Wednesday. Fine to pass if it's still bad timing." Higher hit rate than the original ask.

### Thanking in a way that lands

**Context** — After receiving real help, at work or anywhere. Research by Grant & Gino (JPSP 2010) supports the headline that expressing gratitude motivates further prosocial behavior.

**Reading**
1. "Thanks so much!" said instantly is a social reflex, consumed and forgotten.
2. A specific thank-you names what the helper did and what it changed; it's the version they remember.
3. Public credit (naming their contribution in a channel, a meeting, or a report) converts thanks into an asset for them.

**Response options**
1. Specific + impact: "Your note on the licensing clause saved us from a bad renewal. Genuinely."
2. Public + specific: "The clean migration script was Marcus's; it cut the downtime in half." (Do this in writing, where it persists.)
3. Close the loop even on failure: "Your approach didn't work in the end, but it ruled out the obvious cause. Thanks."

**Pitfalls** — The generic thank-you equal to a receipt; thanking and then never reporting the outcome (helpers want to know how it ended); over-thanking with three follow-up messages, which converts gratitude into obligation to respond.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Thanks for the help" (forgets instantly) vs. "The index you suggested took the report from 40s to 2s. I mentioned it in today's demo." The second one gets you faster help next time.
