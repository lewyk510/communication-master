# Family & Kinship · 家庭、亲戚与人情世故

Chinese-first, Malaysia-based reference for decoding and responding inside the family field: face, guanxi, gifts, elders, pressure, money, conflict, and ritual etiquette across Chinese, Malay, and Indian customs.

### 1. Face (mianzi) inside the family

`Evidence-backed` — 面子 is social standing and dignity; "giving face" (给面子) means showing respect through invitations, praise, deference, and acceptance of hospitality, while causing loss of face publicly is a lasting injury [[raw/sc3-family-kinship/f-lt1-ltl-mianzi.md]]. Inside the family this means: never contradict an elder at the reunion dinner table, redirect criticism away from a relative's spouse or children at gatherings, and publicly credit the host mother-in-law's cooking even when private opinions differ.

A refusal done well preserves face for both sides: give a reason the other person can repeat without embarrassment ("陶医生说这个月要复诊") rather than "我不想". `Practice heuristic`

### 2. Guanxi as family reciprocity

Guanxi networks run on reciprocal favour and staying in contact; sending small gifts or asking small favours keeps a relationship active. In kin terms, the favour ledger runs across generations: 表哥 helped with your university deposit, so your parents expect you to help with his mother's hospital visit. `Evidence-backed` (see general guanxi rule in captures s1-face-guanxi.json digests; specific claim is `Practice heuristic` for the family extension)

Declining a relative's request is read against this ledger. A bare "no" implies the ledger is worthless; a soft "no" that still shows ledger awareness ("这次凑不上，姑婆那边生日我一定到") keeps credit intact. `Practice heuristic`

### 3. Gift-giving rules and taboos

`Evidence-backed` — captured rules: clocks (送钟 sounds like 送终, attending a death), umbrellas (伞 ≈ 散, separation), pears (梨 ≈ 离), white flowers, and the number four (死) are gift taboos; red is auspicious and cash (hongbao) is proper for weddings and new babies; even numbers and "buy local" are the positive side of the list [[raw/sc3-family-kinship/f-lt2-gift-dos.md]].

Malaysia extension `Practice heuristic`: for Malay relatives and friends, gifts and wedding money follow Islamic norms — no alcohol, no pork or non-halal food items, and modest dress; for Indian weddings, cash amounts should end in an odd figure with a plus-one rupee logic (501, 1001) — widely practised, but treat the exact India-to-Malaysia figure rule as `anecdotal` (tier-3 community knowledge, no capture).

### 4. Addressing elders and titles

Chinese kinship terms (姑姑、姨妈、表哥、堂姐) encode generation and side of family; using them correctly gives face to the elder and to the connecting relative. Errors compress respect: calling your father's elder brother's wife "uncle's wife" instead of 大伯母 reads as carelessness. For non-kin elders in Malaysia, common respectful usage: Chinese "uncle"/"auntie" (even to strangers), "Mak Cik"/"Pak Cik" in Malay contexts, "Datuk/Datin" only for actual title-holders. `Practice heuristic`

Operationally: when in doubt, go one degree MORE formal than the crowd; an elder may invite you to relax the title, but you may not self-invite.

### 5. Filial piety: the double edge

`Evidence-backed` — Wei (2023) argues filial piety is a social "passport" that builds trust and family cohesion, but the older model produces blind obedience, moral dilemmas, blurred parent–child boundaries, and neglect of one's own core family; its positive side is companionship and "spiritual care", not merely money [[raw/sc3-family-kinship/f-lt3-filial.md]].

Practical reading: distinguish 报答型 filial actions (visits, calls, paying for treatment — shareable, low-cost to self) from 服从型 demands (quitting a job, breaking a marriage, surrendering all savings). Comply generously with the first; the second is negotiable. `Practice heuristic`

### 6. Negotiating with parents without explosions

`constructed example` framing: the reusable sequence is 让面子 → 认账 → 换方案 → 留台阶.
1. Acknowledge their stake first: "我知道你们是替我担心" (gives face).
2. Anchor on the shared goal: "我也要你们安心".
3. Offer a concrete alternative that treats the real fear: regular video calls, a named doctor, a shared calendar.
4. Leave a step-down path: "再给我一年，如果还不成我再听你们的" (a face-saving retreat clause).

`Practice heuristic`: negotiate in private, on a good day; never at the big dinner, never in front of other relatives who will either back the parent or embarrass them.

### 7. 催婚 / 催生 pressure (marriage and baby nagging)

`Evidence-backed` framing — pressure peaks "before and after children get married" and rides on filial expectations [[raw/sc3-family-kinship/f-lt3-filial.md]]. The nagging is usually a face transaction: your singleton status embarrasses them in front of 小姑 who married her kids off first.

Response ladder `Practice heuristic`:
1. Light deflection + humour (defend no one's face too hard): "还在面试呢，你们帮我留意".
2. Redefine success on values they share: health, career, money.
3. Boundary with the retreat clause (§6 step 4).
4. Enlist an ally elder (a balanced uncle) to absorb the topic at gatherings.
5. Consistent, calm, repetitive lines across years beat a dramatic showdown once.

Response options should never include arguing marriage-equality theory with 大姑妈 at CNY; that is a genuine loss of face for her and escalation for you. `constructed example`

### 8. In-laws

The daily friction: the mother-in-law sets the household 「规矩」, and publicly contradicting her costs face twice over (hers, and your spouse's). `Practice heuristic`: the spouses do the disagreeing privately and the defending publicly; check the unfair load you carry (childcare, cooking) and trade it as a family resource, not a right.

Boundaries with in-laws follow the same 让面子 sequence as §6. Note the exception: never let the boundary talk be delivered by the marrying-in spouse; the biological child delivers it to their own parent. `anecdotal` (community wisdom)

### 9. Siblings and money

The sibling money trap: an eldest is expected to subsidise youngest's education or a brother's business venture; turning it down reads as betraying the ledger even if the project is obviously unwise. Saying "no" requires re-framing: "钱我拿不出，力气我可以出" (time, tutoring, contacts). `Practice heuristic`

Lending: never lend more than you can afford to lose as a gift; treat small intra-family loans as gifts, not investments, but track them, because untracked gives breed both resentment and future "subsidy precedent" claims from other siblings. Split the inherited estate fairly and visibly — invisible fairness breeds suspicion. `anecdotal`

### 10. Family conflict & mediation

`Practice heuristic` map:
- Small skirmish at dinner → de-escalate quickly, don't choose sides, give the loser face ("妈讲也有道理").
- Married-in spouse vs in-law → the biological child mediates, facing both sides privately, in front of the group.
- Long-running sibling feud over money/care of parents → suggest written care roster + quarterly family meeting.
- The voice of 太公/太婆 (grandparents) usually outranks any young couple's issue; win their blessing first.

### 11. Wedding etiquette (Malaysian Chinese / Malay / Indian)

`Evidence-backed` — Malay weddings run through stages including merisik, engagement (pertunangan), akad nikah, and bersanding. Avoid arriving late for the bersanding (the sitting-in-state); bring cash in an appropriate envelope (duit hantaran is the groom's custom; guests give cash — small green/blue packets at Malay weddings is community practice, `anecdotal`); check the invitation card for the reception time before arriving. `Practice heuristic`

Chinese weddings in Malaysia include the tea ceremony; serve in correct seniority order, accept the tea with both hands, and gift even-numbered cash. Indian weddings as temple weddings involve sitting on the floor in modest dress; no shoes inside the temple hall; gift amounts favour figures like 301/501 (`anecdotal`).

### 12. Funeral etiquette (Malaysian multiethnic)

`Evidence-backed` — at Malaysian Chinese funerals, avoid bright colours (especially red, which symbolises celebration), follow wake quiet rules, wear muted colours (black/white/grey/white in Buddhist services, avoid red entirely) [[raw/sc3-family-kinship/f-lt5-funeral.md]]. Condolence money (帛金, white envelopes, odd amounts like 100+1) is customary; sign the condolence book. `Practice heuristic`

Malay funerals: burial within 24h under Islamic practice; go early, offer salam condolences to the family, join the solat jenazah if Muslim (non-Muslims stand respectfully aside). Indian funerals: cremation usual; white clothing, floral garlands; avoid pointing at the body. Confirm the exact details with the specific family's religious channel first — rules vary by denomination. `anecdotal` (for the non-Chinese specifics beyond the captured Chinese page)

### 13. Visiting customs

`Practice heuristic`: arrive with something in hand (fruit, biscuits, kuih); never visit an only-sick household empty-handed; for Chinese New Year visits, bring even-numbered gifts but avoid the four; accept the drink offered (rejecting hospitality is refusing face). Muslim households: remove shoes, dress modestly, don't touch Quran/worship items, don't enter prayer room; during Ramadan, do not arrive near sunset with food in hand. Chinese households during other's festivals: greeting seniors first by title before chatting with peers.

### 14. Caring for ageing parents

`Evidence-backed` — seniors ageing at home are generally more satisfied than those in nursing homes, and after age ~70 expectations of co-living and filial care rise sharply [[raw/sc3-family-kinship/f-lt3-filial.md]]. `Practice heuristic` roster: assign (not volunteer) one sibling as primary medical driver; rotate monthly calls; make money-visible decisions (who pays what) explicit in a family chat so absent siblings see contribution without being humiliated into performing it; keep a parent's decisional role (they choose the doctor, you handle logistics) to preserve their dignity and face.

### 15. Setting boundaries with family

`Practice heuristic`: boundaries in a face culture survive only if they are delivered with the face-saving sequence (§6) and maintained consistently at low heat, not renegotiated in a blow-up. Useful anchors:
1. Frequency (calls / visits) and Money (ceiling per year) are pre-committed in advance, not case-by-case.
2. Separate the decision from the person: "我不同意这个安排" not "你不懂".
3. Remain warm in non-conflict areas (festivals, birthdays) — boundary work does not mean coldness the rest of the year.
4. Marriage/partner decisions and career decisions are non-negotiable over time; keep them off the annual family meeting agenda and say so once, gently, and every year the same.
