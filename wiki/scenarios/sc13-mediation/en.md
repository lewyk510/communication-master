# Mediation · English signals and responses

English-lane entries for third-party mediation: reading what each party is doing to you, and responding in a way that keeps the process neutral. Each entry: Context / Reading / Response options / Pitfalls / Examples. All dialogues are `constructed example` unless traced to `raw/`.

### Both parties ask you to "just say who's right"

**Context** — You are the mutual friend, cousin, close colleague, or group admin pulled into a two-person dispute; both sides start polling you privately.

**Reading** — (1) They genuinely want an arbiter; (2) they want allies and you are a vote; (3) they are testing whether you can afford to offend the other side.

**Response options**
1. Offer structure, not verdict: "I won't rule on this, but I can chair a sit-down where each of you says the full story once."
2. Buy symmetry: "Let me hear your version, theirs, and then I'll say what I understood."
3. Boundaries early: "I can help you talk to each other. I won't carry messages or verdicts between you."

**Pitfalls** — the phrase "in my opinion she's wrong" converts you from facilitator to fourth combatant; triangulation starts with one verdict.

**Examples** — "Can't you just tell him he's being unfair about the renovation noise?" Reply: "I'll tell you both the same thing: let's put a working-hours schedule in writing, Saturday, I'll chair." `constructed example`

### "Fine. It's all my fault. Happy?"

**Context** — Mid-mediation flat voice, folded arms, one side capitulating in a way that ends the conversation rather than settling it.

**Reading** — (1) genuine exhaustion and concession; (2) an avoiding-strategy face-saver, withdrawn inward; (3) throw the concession to watch whether you chase them back.

**Response options**
1. Shrink the sentence: "'All my fault' is too big. Can we scope it to just the parking-hours disagreement?"
2. Move to procedure: ask for one concrete commitment, not one apology.
3. Break and caucus if the tone curdles.

**Pitfalls** — Reading facework capitulation as closure. Verbal agreements without written terms decay in roughly two weeks and resurface with interest.

**Examples** — "Okay fine, it's my fault, I parked wrong." Reply: "I don't need fault. I need: tonight, that car goes to the visitor lot. Yes?" `constructed example`

### "Tell him... but don't say it's from me."

**Context** — One spouse, sibling, or colleague hands you their exact wording plus an anonymity rider, asking you to casually pass it along.

**Reading** — (1) the content is real but face is at risk; (2) a risk transfer, the relay takes the heat; (3) they want the sting of the words passed on, minimising their own accountability.

**Response options**
1. Convert the relay into a three-way meeting: "Say it to my face, I'll chair."
2. Send a written draft, not a whisper: help them write it, then they press send.
3. Refuse the relay explicitly: "I don't pass messages. I can set the table."

**Pitfalls** — Relaying anger verbatim escalates for both sides; every relay distorts, and afterwards you carry a reliability dent.

**Examples** — She texts: "Tell my sister guests can't use the knob of the AC." Reply: "No relays. I've drafted a short note for the family group, in your words, with the timing schedule only. Want to send it yourself?" `constructed example`

### The conflict drags last year's ledger in

**Context** — Parking becomes a wedding-seat grievance becomes a red-packet grievance; the session is now about history.

**Reading** — An emotion valve, not a bargaining agenda. Arguments from the past may justify an entry on a "later list" but cannot be adjudicated in this session.

**Response options**
1. Acknowledge + defer: "I've heard it. It matters. Today we close the car; the ledger goes on a date we book now."
2. Park it in writing: both sides hand their list to a "next session" sheet, visible, not dismissed.
3. Caucus to cool down, then return to one bounded question.

**Pitfalls** — Line-by-line rebuttal of old grievances: the ledger always outlives the mediator's stamina, and every rebuttal gives a new quotable slight.

**Examples** — "And 2019, his side sat at the main table!" Reply: "That goes on the 'later' sheet. Today is parking. First agreed item: which nights count as 'late'?" `constructed example`

### "Whatever you say in here stays in here."

**Context** — You have agreed to be the neutral third party; you are about to take your first private meeting with one side.

**Reading** — The moment to fix ground rules: caucus privacy is the currency of shuttle diplomacy. If it leaks once, no one confides again.

**Response options**
1. Open with the standard assurance (PON wording pattern): "Whatever you tell me here, I won't repeat to the other side unless you approve it. Not even a paraphrase's tone."
2. Ask interest questions, not story-topics: "Why does that deadline matter to you?" — after saying it's to understand, not to challenge them.
3. Separate the confidential from the usable before re-entering: "I have two things, and I need your go-ahead to carry either over."

**Pitfalls** — Taking edgy statements "unofficially" and then using them without approval kills your neutrality faster than taking sides.

**Examples** — In the private slot: "Honestly, the money isn't it. It's that nobody ever said sorry for the water damage." Your check: "May I tell the other side the word 'sorry' matters more than the figure?" `constructed example`

### "It won't work, he proposed it."

**Context** — You suggest a workable fix and team A rejects it; their same-sounding teammates reject it because it came from the other side's mouth.

**Reading** — That's the pattern known as reactive devaluation: ideas lose value when attached to a disfavoured author. Not proof the idea is bad.

**Response options**
1. Borrow your own voice forward: give the idea to the mediator/you and let it be proposed "from the middle".
2. Re-anchor on criteria, not authorship: "Forget who said it. Test it: does it keep both entrances clear?"
3. Let the other side own the flavour: ask them "what would make this acceptable to propose?" and shape it jointly.

**Pitfalls** — Pushing your authorship harder only entrenches it; mediation rewards developing the idea via the neutral chair.

**Examples** — "My suggestion is a shared space after 8 pm." Bloc A: "Of course you'd say that." So you hand it to the group chat as "the middle's cut", where both parties refine it. `constructed example`

### Two neighbours, one shared wall

**Context** — Noise, water drips, guest parking, cat, renovation timing. Both live nearby for a decade; what happens here sets the tone for a decade more.

**Reading** — The dispute's monetary value is trivial but the relationship bet is large; neither a court nor a Facebook rant produces endurable neighbours.

**Response options**
1. Joint session with a warm-up: start from the shared history of the street ("we've both kept this block quiet for ten years").
2. Interest-first: replace "he parks too close" with each side's concrete need (visit access, van clearance, elderly-mother lift).
3. Land a written, dated, minimal protocol: quiet hours, notice for renovations, contact first instead of banging on a wall.

**Pitfalls** — Trying to file a verdict ("you're the worse neighbour") buys one calm month of drummed-up future grievances.

**Examples** — The wall drips. You: "Fix is one weekend. Where we go from here, rules: which bucket, which morning, who paints?" They sign a note both keep. `constructed example`

### "We tried that already, it didn't hold."

**Context** — This is round two or three of the same dispute; both sides recite the previous collapse into the agenda.

**Reading** — (1) the previous round had no written record, so nothing was binding; (2) an unspoken interest was never surfaced; (3) one side intends delay, not agreement.

**Response options**
1. Only close rounds in writing: end the session with a signed text or group message listing every agreed clause.
2. Hunt the hidden interest in caucus: "What were you afraid would happen if we actually agreed last time?"
3. Set an escalation path: if nothing is written after this round, it moves to a formal channel (in Kuala Lumpur, the DBKL community mediation centre runs a seven-step process).

**Pitfalls** — Running identical round tables again; the fourth round of an unwritten agreement is theatre.

**Examples** — Round three ends with a phone note both families screenshot: "Weekday noise off by 10 pm; renovation days announced in the group; disputes go straight to the chair, not to shouting." `constructed example`

### Boss vs staff: the power asymmetry warning

**Context** — A junior asks whether mediation makes sense when the other party holds write-up leverage, rosters, and references.

**Reading** — (1) They fear retaliation more than they want resolution; (2) they want someone to stop the pressure, which is a protective process and not mediation; (3) the dispute touches pay, dismissal, or harassment, and mediation is wrong.

**Response options**
1. Route it: anything about wages, dismissal, bullying, or harassment goes to HR's formal process or the statutory channel, not a living-room chat.
2. Symmetrize what remains: HR chairs a recorded three-way meeting; both sides submit one written statement first; a two-week review point is set in the calendar, not assumed.
3. Refer to professionals: legal advice, union, or counselling when personal safety or legal rights are at stake.

**Pitfalls** — Acting as a private "mediator" where retaliation risk exists hands the powerful side the weaker side's floor price, with no paper trail for the weaker side.

**Examples** — "Can we just talk it out with Ian in the room?" Reply: "Not informal. HR chairs it, both statements written, and we fix a check-in in two weeks before either of you hears 'no change' again." `constructed example`
