# Friends & Social · English signal handbook

Written from the English-speaking adult friendship context: group-chat silence, the vague maybe, the friend who only calls with an agenda. Entries are natively authored and not translated from zh/ms.

### "We should totally hang out soon!"

**Context** — said at the end of a party, a chance meeting, or in a comment thread. Low stakes, high vagueness.

**Reading**
1. Polite exit line: it means goodbye and nothing more.
2. Genuine interest without initiative: they'd come if you organise it.
3. A real invitation missing only a date.

**Response options**
1. Convert it on the spot: "Yes! Are you free Thursday after work? There's a new ramen place." A real one answers, a politeness evaporates.
2. Bounce the initiative back: "Definitely. Pick a day and I'll be there." You learn who actually initiates.
3. Match the register: "For sure, see you around." No ledger, no resentment.

**Pitfalls** — treating politeness as a promise and silently keeping score; treating a real offer as politeness and brushing it off, so the other person never tries again.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: at a farewell party someone says "we should hang out soon." You reply "Thursday 7pm, the new ramen place?" They reply with a thumbs up and a time. First real catch-up in two years happens Thursday.

### "I'll see if I can make it"

**Context** — the classic soft no on a WhatsApp group when you've shared an event. Common with mid-tier friends.

**Reading**
1. Genuine maybe: they're checking another commitment first.
2. Decline with no exit wound: this is how adults say no here.
3. Stalling until the deadline decides for them.

**Response options**
1. Close the loop gently: "No worries, let me know by Friday either way, so I can book." A deadline is a kindness to both sides.
2. Ask once, directly: "Rough yes or rough no?" Many maybes are just people avoiding the word no.
3. Let it go: after the deadline, update the headcount without commentary.

**Pitfalls** — chasing the maybe for days ("so are you coming?"), which turns your event into a chore; reading every soft no as rejection and striking the person off, when a single no means nothing.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: you post a futsal Saturday invite. Three "I'll see"s. You write "Final headcount Friday 6pm." Two confirm, one goes quiet, you book for ten, everyone survives.

### "Can you pay me back for dinner?"

**Context** — chasing a small debt: RM38 here, RM12.50 there. Channels: bank transfer app, group chat, or an awkward hallway mention. The friendship risk is in the chasing, not the amount.

**Reading**
1. They genuinely forgot: most small debts are memory problems, not character problems.
2. They hope you'll let it slide and you're the one who has to name it.
3. They're tight this month and embarrassed to say so.

**Response options**
1. Make it frictionless: "Just sent my bank details, whenever it's convenient this week." Low pressure, concrete, easy.
2. Bundle it forward: "No rush, but grab the next one and we're square." Turns a chase into a ritual.
3. Name the pattern once, privately, if it repeats: "Small thing, but I've noticed I'm always the one floating. Can we do pay-as-we-go?"

**Pitfalls** — public callouts in the group chat, which converts a RM38 debt into a status war; apologising while asking ("sorry to be annoying but..."), which teaches them your boundary is optional.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: after a bill you paid, a friend goes quiet on your payment request for nine days. You send the bank details again with "this week whenever, no rush". Paid that night. Nobody mentions the nine days.

### "It was just a joke, don't be so sensitive"

**Context** — banter that landed wrong: in the group chat, at the mamak table, at a party. The teaser defends, others watch to see what you do.

**Reading**
1. Real surprise: they truly didn't know the line was there.
2. Deflection: they knew, and this is armour.
3. Audience management: they care more about the room laughing than about you.

**Response options**
1. Hold the line lightly, once: "Maybe, but that one's not funny to me. Different topic." No lecture, no exit from the evening.
2. Use the third-party route: say nothing publicly, text them later: "That joke about my job, let's not." Most people correct privately.
3. Recalibrate distance if it repeats: a friend who needs you as their audience has told you their price of admission.

**Pitfalls** — laughing along while it stings, which is a signature on the joke's legitimacy; exploding on the spot, which hands the room a new, better joke: you.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: at dinner a friend jokes about your break-up for the third time. You say "That one's retired now." He blinks, says "fair", and changes topic. The next dinner, no jokes about it.

### "Don't tell anyone, but..."

**Context** — a friend lowers their voice to share something about a third person. kopitiam, late-night call, or a stairwell at a wedding.

**Reading**
1. They need a vault, not a megaphone: pure venting.
2. A loyalty test: they're measuring what you do with sensitive information.
3. Proxy messaging: they want someone told, without their name on it.

**Response options**
1. Price the information before accepting: "Happy to listen. Anything involving other people, I don't pass on and I don't pick sides." Set terms first, receive second.
2. Refuse the megaphone role but keep the friend: "I won't repeat it, but I'm also not going to pretend I didn't hear it. What are you going to do?"
3. Slide off pure entertainment gossip: "I'll skip that one. What else is new with you?"

**Pitfalls** — accepting first and asking what it is second, which leaves you holding something you can't put down; repeating it "just to one person, who won't tell anyone", the exact sentence everyone says before a leak.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Don't tell anyone, but Wei Ming's company is doing retrenchments." You reply "Got it, stays with me." It stays. When the news breaks publicly, your friend trusts you with the next secret too.

### "Have you heard about..." (being handed gossip)

**Context** — someone tries to hand you information about a mutual friend's private life. Research from UC Berkeley (Feinberg, Willer and colleagues) found prosocial gossip has a real function: warning others about untrustworthy people calms the teller and makes cheaters behave. The same study is no licence for entertainment gossip.

**Reading**
1. Prosocial warning: they think you're about to be exploited, and they might be right.
2. Social currency: they're buying closeness with you at someone else's cost.
3. Fishing: they want to see your reaction before deciding what you are to them.

**Response options**
1. If it's a genuine warning: accept facts and sources only. "Thank you, seriously. Where did you see this?" No embellishment when you pass it on.
2. If it's entertainment: decline without judging them. "That's their business. I'd want the same grace."
3. If the gossip is about you: skip the defence tour. Ask the source once: "Where did this come from?" Then correct it where it actually matters.

**Pitfalls** — moralising at the teller, which turns you into the next topic; repeating a warning without its source, which converts protection into rumour.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: a friend warns you a group-buy organiser has vanished with money before. You check, hold your deposit, and quietly tell two members "ask for proof of purchase first". Nobody loses money. That's the function of gossip done properly.

### "Should we just split it evenly?"

**Context** — the moment the bill lands at a table of six with wildly different orders. Malaysia's answer is often a fight for the bill; the international default is the even split.

**Reading**
1. Practical: even split is fastest and the difference doesn't matter to them.
2. Self-serving: they ordered the lamb and are hoping the group values speed.
3. Ritual: the "even split" is a polite fiction and everyone really pays for what they ate.

**Response options**
1. Set the script in advance for regular groups: "Usual rule: we split evenly, drinks separate." Negotiate once, never again.
2. Split by consumption without drama when the gap is real: "I'll work it out on the app, one minute." A calculator is more persuasive than a debate.
3. Eat the difference under RM20 quietly; name anything bigger.

**Pitfalls** — silently overpaying for the big eater three times, then exploding over RM45; fighting over the bill so hard that nobody enjoys the meal you're supposedly celebrating.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: bill RM520, six people. One had two mocktails and a salad, another had steak. You post in the chat: "Split by item, total RM520, your share in the sheet. Even split next time if we all order similarly." Two eye-rolls, zero enemies.

### "Are you free this weekend?" (the ambush invite)

**Context** — a friend asks about your availability before saying what for. Common prelude to moving help, wedding prep, or a long car ride.

**Reading**
1. Cautious inviting: they're checking before investing in details.
2. Ambush: the "free" is a labour enquiry, not a hangout.
3. Genuinely flexible: they'll fit whatever you want to do.

**Response options**
1. Ask before answering: "Free-ish. What's the plan?" Never commit to a blank slot; a yes to "are you free" is a yes to whatever follows.
2. Answer with a conditional: "Saturday morning is open. If you need help moving, I can do 8 to 11, lunch on me after."
3. If the plan appears and it's not for you: decline the plan, not the friendship. "That I'll skip, but Saturday lunch sounds good."

**Pitfalls** — saying yes to a blank calendar slot and discovering you've volunteered for a six-hour IKEA run; feeling cheated afterwards and going cold, when a single clarifying question would have prevented it all.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Are you free this Saturday?" "Morning is, what's up?" "Help me move my sofa down." "8 to 11 and I'll buy you lunch." Both parties feel like they won.

### "No worries if not!"

**Context** — the softener attached to almost every modern ask: a favour, a lift, a plus-one slot. It protects the asker and hands all the discomfort to the receiver.

**Reading**
1. Genuine politeness: they mean it, and a no costs nothing.
2. Pressure in a friendly costume: the ask is real, the exit is decoration.
3. Self-soothing: they're managing their own fear of rejection.

**Response options**
1. Answer the ask, not the softener: either "Yes, I can do that" or "Can't this time, sorry." Don't match vagueness with vagueness.
2. Counter-offer when you want to help differently: "Can't do Friday, but Saturday morning works."
3. If you're the asker, make the exit real: give a deadline, accept the first no, never re-ask the same week.

**Pitfalls** — reading "no worries if not" as permission to chase; treating every soft ask as manipulation, which makes you refuse people who meant it sincerely.

**Examples** — `constructed example`: "Any chance you could take my shift Tuesday? No worries if not!" You reply "Can't Tuesday, Thursday works if that helps." Trade done, friendship intact, nobody owes anyone an apology.
