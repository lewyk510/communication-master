# Negotiation & Deals · English signals and responses

English-lane entries for negotiation signals, tactics, and counters. Every entry carries Context / Reading / Response options / Pitfalls / Examples. Dialogues are `constructed example` unless captured in raw/.

### "That's our best price."

**Context** — Car dealers, furniture stores, contractors, and HR all use this scripted phrase early in a negotiation.

**Reading** — Three candidates: (1) genuine floor, thin-margin seller; (2) scripted defense, one more round often frees 5%; (3) a probe to see if you know the market.

**Response options**
1. Move to structure: "What does that number break down into?"
2. Offer volume or terms instead: "What if I take two, or pay upfront?"
3. Name the walk-away quietly: "I'll keep it in mind. I've got one more quote to compare."

**Pitfalls** — Reading it as a hard no and leaving (it rarely is on the first pass); or grinding rudely at a genuinely thin-margin seller and burning the relationship.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "That's our best price." / "Understood. What does it look like if I pay cash today?"

### The flinch

**Context** — You state your price or hear theirs; the other side visibly recoils, gasps, or goes wide-eyed.

**Reading** — (1) Rehearsed tactic, not genuine shock; (2) real surprise, meaning your number is genuinely out of their range; (3) opening move of a longer squeeze. Evidence-backed: Fassina & Whyte (2014, cited in the NCMR review) found negotiators who used a strategic flinch got significantly better results, though an intense flinch damaged the relationship more.

**Response options**
1. Stay anchored: "Is that surprise about the number itself, or the terms around it?"
2. Mirror their move at low intensity.
3. Recenter on criteria: "Let's set the theater aside and check the comparable prices together."

**Pitfalls** — Lowering your number just because someone winced; over-flinching yourself, which reads as disrespect.

**Examples** — `constructed example` You: "Total is 4,500." Them: *whistles* "That's steep." You: "Steep compared to what benchmark?"

### "I need to check with my partner/boss."

**Context** — The stall used by buyers and salespeople alike when they don't want to commit on the spot.

**Reading** — (1) True, decisions are genuinely shared; (2) a polite no with a delay; (3) a manufactured veto so they can come back with "they said no to your price."

**Response options**
1. Bind the absent party in advance: "Great. What would your partner need to see to say yes?"
2. Ask for a decision window: "When will you two have talked? I'll follow up Thursday."
3. Give ammunition: send a one-page recap they can forward.

**Pitfalls** — Treating it as a rejection and chasing with immediate discounts; forgetting that the person in the room is the one you actually have to convince first.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "I'll need to ask my wife." / "Sure. What's the one thing she'd push back on, the price or the timing?"

### The nibble

**Context** — All terms are agreed, pens are hovering, and a small extra request appears: "You'll throw in delivery, right?"

**Reading** — Almost always a nibble: an add-on demanded after your exit cost rises. Habit more than malice.

**Response options**
1. Trade it symmetrically: "Happy to. That brings the total to 4,650."
2. Reopen the sheet: "Sure, let me print the updated quote with delivery included."
3. Decline with a smile: "Everything agreed stays agreed. Next round, raise it during the negotiation."

**Pitfalls** — Nodding along and teaching the other side that post-deal requests are free; killing the deal over a $20 nibble when goodwill matters.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "Can you include the oil change too?" / "I can. Updated total is 4,650, all in."

### Good cop / bad cop

**Context** — Two negotiators on the other side: one plays harsh, one plays friendly, and the friendly one's offer starts feeling like relief.

**Reading** — (1) Staged routine; (2) genuine internal disagreement, which is useful information; (3) the "good cop" terms are the real script, dressed up as generosity.

**Response options**
1. Talk issues, not roles: "Let's treat both of you as one voice. What's the actual bottom line?"
2. Mine the bad cop: the harsh terms often map the real internal floor.
3. Negotiate with the good cop only: "If that package works for you, we have a deal today."

**Pitfalls** — Gratitude toward the friendly one overriding your own numbers; forgetting you can ask to speak to the actual decision-maker.

**Examples** — `constructed example` Bad cop: "This is insulting." Good cop: "Look, just accept our standard terms." You: "Which of the two positions is the company's?"

### "Take it or leave it."

**Context** — An ultimatum late in the negotiation, common in job offers and property.

**Reading** — (1) A genuine reservation-price statement, ZOPA is exhausted; (2) a pressure device, "leave it" often softens if you ask one more calibrated question; (3) an attempt to skip the haggling phase entirely.

**Response options**
1. Test it calmly: "I hear you. Before I decide, what happens to the signing bonus if we close this week?"
2. Accept the frame but add a term: "I'll take the number. Then let's agree the review happens in six months, not twelve."
3. Use your BATNA: "Then I'll take the other offer. It's been a good conversation."

**Pitfalls** — Believing the ultimatum is final without checking; burning the relationship while testing it, the test should be soft, the exit ready.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "4,200. Take it or leave it." / "Taken, if the review cycle is six months. Otherwise I'll pass."

### Silence after your offer

**Context** — You state a price; the room goes quiet on a call or across the table.

**Reading** — (1) They are calculating, hold your line; (2) deliberate pressure, waiting for you to crack; (3) in Malaysian business settings, discomfort being filled by small talk rather than calculation, silence reads differently here than in US-style negotiation.

**Response options**
1. Say nothing. First person to fill the void usually concedes (practice heuristic).
2. Break it with a label: "It sounds like that number needs a conversation on your side."
3. Set a return channel: "I'll email the quote. Give me your answer by tomorrow noon."

**Pitfalls** — Filling your own silence with a discount; in Malaysia, misreading polite chat as weakness, per the trade.gov guide, frankness and relationship contact are both normal here.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "The total is 4,500." (15 seconds of silence) "...4,500 works if delivery is on you."

### "Is that the best you can do?"

**Context** — The counterparty's direct squeeze, heard at markets, dealerships, and salary talks.

**Reading** — (1) A routine prompt, they expect a concession exists; (2) a genuine close signal, they want one last reason to say yes; (3) a test of your confidence and whether your number moves under pressure.

**Response options**
1. Small structured give: "I can do 4,400 if you sign both years today."
2. Hold and trade: "That is my best on price. I can improve the warranty instead."
3. Flip to a calibrated question: "What would make this work for you at 4,300?"

**Pitfalls** — Instantly producing a better number without getting anything back, you just taught them your first offer was fake; answering with a flat "yes" that kills momentum when they wanted one more win.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "Is that the best you can do?" / "On price, yes. On the delivery date, I have room."

### Anchor then meet in the middle

**Context** — Classic counter to an aggressive first offer: they anchor at 1,000 hoping for a split at 750.

**Reading** — The midpoint-of-anchors pattern: whoever anchors harder pulls the "compromise" toward themselves. Evidence-backed in the NCMR review: first offers strongly pull final outcomes (Galinsky & Mussweiler 2001 reported roughly a .697 correlation between initial offers and outcomes).

**Response options**
1. Refuse the midpoint: "The middle of two numbers isn't the market. Let's use comparable sales."
2. Counter-anchor with data and reasons attached.
3. Expand the pie with a MESO-style set: two or three equivalent packages, letting their choice reveal priorities (Leonardelli et al. 2019, cited in NCMR).

**Pitfalls** — Splitting the difference reflexively; treating their anchor as the reference point in your head after rejecting it out loud.

**Examples** — `constructed example` They: "1,000." You: "Market comparables put it at 620. I can go 640 with a two-year contract."

### "Let's just do it verbally first."

**Context** — Vendor arrangements, renovation jobs, informal deals: start now, paperwork later, or never.

**Reading** — (1) Trust culture, normal among long-term contacts in Malaysia; (2) avoidance of documentation for tax or liability reasons; (3) intent to keep terms flexible, which usually means favorable to whoever renegotiates later.

**Response options**
1. Accept verbally, then send a recap: "I'll WhatsApp you the scope and price now so we're aligned."
2. Stage the paper: "Let's do a one-pager for phase one, keep it simple."
3. Ask the calibrated question: "How should we handle it if we remember the scope differently in week three?"

**Pitfalls** — Months of drift where each side "remembers" different terms; discovering at dispute time that nothing except memories exists.

**Examples** — `constructed example` "We'll sort the details later, verbal is fine." / "Fine by me. I'm sending the scope and price on WhatsApp now so we don't misremember."
