### Cold-calling someone who hates calls

**Context** — You have to phone a client, landlord, or colleague who belongs to the "text-first" generation. Nearly 60% of surveyed Gen Z dread calls, and unsolicited calls read as rude ambushes (Evidence-backed, CommBank 2023 via The Conversation, see core.md).

**Reading** — 1) The call itself is the offense, before you say a word; 2) An unknown number triggers "something is wrong" dread; 3) If it turns out non-urgent, they feel ambushed and annoyed.

**Response options**
1. Text first: "Got 10 min for a call about the lease? No rush." Then call at the agreed time.
2. Must call cold: open with "Sorry to call out of the blue, one minute, feel free to hang up."
3. They don't pick up: leave a 20-second voicemail, then one text with the same content.

**Pitfalls** — Calling twice in a row with no voicemail; treating "they didn't pick up" as rudeness when it's the new default.

**Examples** — "Hi, this is Kim from the clinic — did I catch you at a bad time? I need two minutes to confirm your Friday slot." constructed example.

### Leaving a voicemail that gets returned

**Context** — They didn't pick up. You need a callback, and you have 20 seconds of their attention at most.

**Reading** — Voicemails are treated as low-priority by everyone under 50; the only ones that get returned are short, structured, and name the next step. The "text first, never leave a voicemail" norm is spreading even among etiquette traditionalists (anecdotal, per Emily Post podcast snapshot in raw captures).

**Response options**
1. Five elements in order: your name and number, time and context, one-line message, explicit ask, number repeated.
2. Tone matters, not just content: speak 10% slower and smile while talking; it's audible.
3. No voicemail needed? Hang up and text the same three lines instead; it's searchable.

**Pitfalls** — Reading the number once, fast; burying the ask at minute two; leaving details (amounts, dates) in voice only, where they can't be searched or quoted.

**Examples** — "Hi Mr. Rahman, James here, 012-345-6789. Calling Tuesday about the signed contract; please call me back today if you can. Again, 012-345-6789." constructed example.

### "Sorry, you're on mute"

**Context** — Someone talks for 30 seconds to a frozen screen; or you unmute and hear your own echo played back as a comment.

**Reading** — Purely technical, zero social meaning. What people judge is the recovery, not the accident.

**Response options**
1. If it was you: no apology tour. Just restate the point: "Okay, now you can hear me. As I was saying..."
2. Alerting others: "You might be on mute." Facts only, no giggling.
3. Host move: first 30 seconds, everyone says "can hear you" in turn; burns the failure early and cheap.

**Pitfalls** — Delivering a two-minute apology for a two-second accident; reminding the same person five times without telling them where the button is.

**Examples** — "You're on mute." → click → "Sorry, thanks. The budget number is 12k, not 21k." Ten seconds, done. constructed example.

### Camera on or off: the standing debate

**Context** — You join a meeting; eight faces, two black tiles. Or you're the black tile wondering if you should switch on.

**Reading** — 1) Black tile: weak network, a bad hair day, or deliberate fatigue management; 2) Cameras-off lowers cognitive load, cameras-on supports connection; policies should match the meeting type, not blanket rules (Evidence-backed direction from corporate meeting analytics, tier 3 vendor, see core.md); 3) Peers rarely judge; hosts do.

**Response options**
1. One-on-ones, interviews, small reviews: camera on, it's your engagement signal.
2. Large broadcasts and status meetings: off is fine; stay visibly active in chat.
3. If you stay off repeatedly: tell the host once ("audio-only this week, sick kid at home"), so silence doesn't read as disengagement.

**Pitfalls** — Reading every black tile as disrespect; demanding cameras on while running the meeting off-camera yourself.

**Examples** — Host: "Cameras optional today; turn on when you speak." Attendance and chat participation both rise. constructed example.

### Eye contact is a camera, not a face

**Context** — You're presenting on a video call and feel connected, looking at your listener's face on screen. They later say you seemed distracted.

**Reading** — Looking at the screen feels natural to you but reads as looking down to them. The eye-contact channel on video is the camera lens, not the display; this is the standard rule across meeting-etiquette guides (Practice heuristic, see core.md).

**Response options**
1. While speaking: look at the camera. Put a sticky note with an arrow next to it.
2. While listening: look at the screen freely; nod visibly to compensate for the lost signal.
3. Hardware fix: raise the laptop so the camera sits at eye level; one fix improves framing and gaze together.

**Pitfalls** — Reading your own thumbnail instead of the audience (hide self-view); mounting the camera so low that you appear to loom or droop.

**Examples** — In a client pitch, the presenter tapes an arrow beside the webcam and looks there when delivering numbers. The client reports "she really looked at us." constructed example.

### Talking over each other on a conference call

**Context** — Four people on an audio-only bridge. Two start at once; the quietest person never speaks again.

**Reading** — Audio calls have no eye contact or gesture cues to manage turn-taking, so collisions are mechanical, not rude. But hosts and bosses will read silence as "no contribution."

**Response options**
1. On collision: "Go ahead, I'll follow." Yielding wins goodwill in seconds.
2. Always open with your name on audio-only: "This is Priya, one addition..." Voices have no identity.
3. Host: invite the silent by name, and close each agenda point by restating the decision and owner.

**Pitfalls** — A five-second "no no, you go" dance that ends with both silent; leaving a call where three people made commitments nobody wrote down.

**Examples** — Two voices collide; B says "Go ahead, I'll add mine at the end," then does, by name. The host logs both points. constructed example.

### Screen-sharing gone wrong

**Context** — You share your full desktop to present; a WhatsApp popup or a job-hunt tab flashes for half a second.

**Reading** — The audience logs the flash, not the chart. Full-screen share broadcasts your whole digital life, so the risk is scope, not intent.

**Response options**
1. Before: enable Do Not Disturb, close stray tabs, share the single window instead of the desktop.
2. Trivial slip: keep talking; explanation amplifies it.
3. Sensitive slip: stop sharing immediately, "Sorry, wrong window," move on. Never try to scroll-past it live.

**Pitfalls** — Assuming notifications are paused when they aren't; deleting the embarrassing tab history afterwards in view of the same audience.

**Examples** — Mid-pitch, an email preview reading "salary negotiation tips" pops up. She stops the share, says "wrong window, next slide please," and continues. constructed example.

### Zoom fatigue: spotting it and cutting it

**Context** — By 3 p.m. your eyes sting, you reread the same comment, and you dread the 3:30 call. Stanford's Jeremy Bailenson traced this to four mechanics: excessive close-up eye contact, seeing yourself in real time, locked-down mobility, and inflated cognitive load (Evidence-backed, *Technology, Mind and Behavior* 2021, see core.md).

**Reading** — 1) The medium, not you, is the problem: "just because you can use video doesn't mean you have to"; 2) Fatigue shows up socially as ghost-mode attendance: camera off, muted, contributing nothing; 3) Teams treat it as a personal failing when it's a design cost.

**Response options**
1. Shrink the window, use an external keyboard, and hide self-view after checking your frame.
2. Take "audio-only breaks" in long meetings, and turn your body away from the screen while you do.
3. Meeting owners: cut status meetings to async notes, cap calls at 50 minutes, and make camera use optional by default.

**Pitfalls** — Powering through back-to-back calls and calling it productivity; fixing fatigue with "more willpower" when the fixes are physical and one-click.

**Examples** — A team lead moves the weekly broadcast to a recorded note, keeps one 30-minute discussion call, and allows camera-off. Fatigue complaints stop within a month. constructed example.

### Ending a call without dangling

**Context** — Business is done but the call keeps breathing. No one stands up to leave; there is no door.

**Reading** — Calls lack physical exit signals, so they drag unless someone verbalizes the close. An abrupt end without a summary wastes the whole call.

**Response options**
1. Three-step close: recap the decision, name the next step, end warm: "So I'll send the draft Friday, you return it Monday. Thanks, this helped a lot."
2. They keep going: "I'll let you go" hands them the exit with dignity.
3. Peer calls: the caller closes; with seniors or clients: wait for them, or offer "I know you're busy."

**Pitfalls** — Ending on a sour line ("do better next time"), which becomes the version they remember; faking a bad signal to escape your boss.

**Examples** — "Great, summary: option A, draft Friday. Anyway, I'll let you go. Thanks, Sarah." constructed example.

### Titles on Malaysian business calls

**Context** — You're calling a Malaysian client whose card reads "Dato' Ahmad" or "Tan Sri Lee." Titles here are federal or state honors with real hierarchy: Tun above Tan Sri above Datuk/Dato' (Evidence-backed, see MDDM capture in core.md).

**Reading** — Using the right title signals respect and local literacy; skipping it reads as either ignorance or deliberate slight. If a title exists, "Encik" is a demotion.

**Response options**
1. Card shows a title: open with "Dato' Ahmad, good morning," title plus name, never the bare title.
2. Unsure: start with Encik or Puan plus name, and let the secretary's answer or the card correct you upward.
3. Ministers and lawmakers take Yang Berhormat (anecdotal, tier 3 wiki); ambassadors take Tuan Yang Terutama.

**Pitfalls** — Assuming Dato' and Datuk are interchangeable (different grantors); inventing titles to flatter, which is worse than using none.

**Examples** — "Selamat pagi, Dato' Ahmad. This is James from Meridian, two minutes to confirm Wednesday's quotation." constructed example.
